# Q13 — Industry sector: emission sources, solutions, challenges, and progress against targets?

India already melts more steel with electricity than with coal fire. It also makes the dirtiest
steel of any large producer on earth. Both are true at the same time, and the reason is the whole
story of this sector: here the fuel is not only the fire. It is also the ingredient.

## 1. Where the carbon comes from

**Industry is 21.7 percent of everything India emits** — the second-biggest source, behind only
electricity. But "industry" is not one thing. Two completely different problems hide inside that
number, and telling them apart is the key to everything that follows.

- **Heat is an energy problem.** A factory needs fierce heat, and today it makes that heat by
  burning coal or gas. Clean electricity can do the same job — but only if you rebuild the furnace.
  Aluminium already proves it: melting it needs over 1,000 °C, and India does that with electricity
  today.
- **Feedstock is a chemistry problem.** Here the fossil fuel is not the fire. It is the
  **ingredient**. Coal is what pulls the oxygen out of iron ore. Clean electricity cannot help,
  because electricity is not a material you can make things out of.

Now the four places the carbon actually comes from, biggest first.

| Where it comes from | Tonnes a year | Of India | The problem inside it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1.1 Steel** | 240–300 Mt | **6–7%** | **Both** — fierce heat, and coal as the ingredient |
| **1.2 Cement** | about 246 Mt | **about 6%** | **Both** — and 56% comes out of the limestone itself |
| **1.3 Plastics and fertiliser** | 70–90 Mt *(our estimate)* | **about 2%** | **Mostly chemistry** — the fuel is the raw material |
| **1.4 Everything else** | the remainder | **about 7%** | **Mostly heat**, and mostly the easier, lower heat |

Row 1.4 is bricks, glass, aluminium, textiles and food. Steel is India's single biggest factory
source, and cement is close behind — together about 13 percent of everything the country emits, from
just two products.

Two of those rows carry almost all the weight. India is the world's **second-largest steel maker**
and the **largest maker of sponge iron**, and Indian steel gives off **2.54 tonnes of carbon for
every tonne of steel** against a world average of 1.91 — higher because it leans on coal, about
85% of which is imported. India is also the world's **second-largest cement maker**, at roughly
450 million tonnes a year.

Plastics sit third rather than first, and the reason is worth stating plainly. India's plastic use
**per person** is far below the world average. The total is large only because there are 1.4
billion people.

## 2. The solutions, ranked

Ranked by the same test used for electricity: **how many tonnes of carbon it removes per year, at
today's price and today's build time.**

> **Two words worth knowing first.** **Clinker** is the grey lump you get by cooking limestone in a
> kiln — it is the part of cement that carries almost all the carbon. **Sponge iron** is iron made
> without melting it, by using coal or gas to pull the oxygen out of the ore. India makes more of
> it than any country on earth.

- **2.1 Put less clinker in the cement.** Mix in leftovers instead — ash from coal power stations,
  slag from steel plants. No new factory needed. India is already good at this: for every tonne of
  cement it makes, only about **0.71 tonnes is clinker**, one of the lowest figures in the world.
  The industry's own plan is to reach **0.56 by 2070**.
- **2.2 Melt old scrap steel instead of making new iron.** Scrap needs no ore, no coal and no
  chemistry — just heat. And India already owns the machines: **117 million tonnes** of electric
  furnace capacity against **88.5 million tonnes** of blast furnaces.
- **2.3 Swap the flame for electricity wherever the heat is not extreme.** Roughly **half of all
  industrial energy is needed below 500 °C** — boiling, drying, steaming, washing. Food, textiles,
  paper. That heat is easy to make with electricity today.
- **2.4 Make iron with natural gas instead of coal.** A stepping stone, not a destination. The
  Ministry of Steel's own figures: making steel this way gives off **1.40 to 1.60 tonnes of carbon
  per tonne of steel**, against **2.70 to 3.10 tonnes** for the coal route India mostly uses. It
  uses the same shaft furnace shape India already knows, and that same vessel can later be switched
  to hydrogen.
- **2.5 Make iron with hydrogen.** The real fix. Hydrogen can do the exact job coal does — pull
  oxygen out of iron ore — and what comes out is water instead of carbon dioxide. The same hydrogen
  also cleans up fertiliser, which is made from the same molecule.
- **2.6 Catch the carbon at the cement chimney.** Last, and unavoidable. Nothing above touches the
  carbon that comes out of limestone itself. Only catching it does.

**Why the order, and the one line that matters.** Look at what each solution attacks. **2.1 to 2.4
all deal with the fire.** They are cheaper, faster and available now, and together they can take
India a long way. **Only 2.5 and 2.6 touch the chemistry** — the coal that is an ingredient, and
the carbon locked inside limestone. Those two are the expensive, slow, barely-started ones.
**India can buy the easy half today. The hard half has no cheap answer yet.**

## 3. The challenge behind each

Same six, same order.

**3.1 Blended cement — India has already taken most of this win.**
This is the cheapest tonne in the sector, which is exactly the problem: India has largely spent it.
Going below 0.71 gets harder fast. The substitutes are somebody else's leftovers — ash exists only
because coal plants burn, slag only because steel is made — so the supply is capped by other
industries, not by demand. Push the blend too far and the concrete sets slower and starts weaker,
which builders will not accept. And even a perfect blend leaves the limestone problem untouched.

**3.2 Scrap steel — a young country has not thrown away its steel yet.**
India used **41 million tonnes** of scrap in 2024-25 and had to **import about a quarter of it**.
Steel sits inside buildings, bridges and vehicles for thirty to fifty years before anyone scraps
it. India's steel is still in use, holding up buildings that are new. So India's own former steel
secretary puts the ceiling plainly: **only about 25% of India's steel demand can ever be met from
scrap** — and even by 2050 India is expected to be short of **40 to 50 million tonnes of scrap a
year**. Short of the scrap, not of the steel. The furnaces are there. The thing to feed them is not.

**3.3 Electric heat — it lands right back on the grid.**
Two problems, one physical and one borrowed. The physical one: a flame and an electric element do
not deliver heat the same way, so you cannot unbolt the burner and bolt on a heater — the vessel
and often the whole line must be rebuilt. The borrowed one: every unit of that heat now comes
through a wire. On a grid still carrying about **0.71 kg of carbon in every unit**, electrifying a
furnace moves the carbon rather than removing it. Industry's heat problem is the power question
wearing a different hat.

**3.4 Gas as a stepping stone — there is no pipe, and there is no money.**
Nothing is wrong with the technology. Two other things are wrong.

- **No pipe.** Only **21% of India's blast furnace capacity and 5% of its coal-based sponge iron
  capacity** sits within 25 kilometres of a gas pipeline. For nineteen plants in twenty, the price
  of gas never even comes up.
- **No money.** Where there is a pipe, the bill still bites. Gas makes **2.3% of India's
  electricity** against **21.8% worldwide**, and India's gas power capacity has actually shrunk,
  from 25,329 megawatts in 2017 to 20,122 today. Those plants run only about **28% of the time**.
  A steelmaker reads the same price sheet a power company does.

A bridge with no road to it, and a toll nobody can pay.

**3.5 Hydrogen iron — four separate walls, not one.**

- **Price.** Green hydrogen in India costs **₹397 to ₹560 a kilo**. The grey hydrogen it must
  replace costs **₹150 to ₹200**. Two to three times, on a product sold on price.
- **The ore.** Making iron this way needs unusually pure ore. **Most of India's iron ore does not
  qualify** without extra cleaning, which costs more again.
- **The power.** It needs a very large, steady, round-the-clock clean supply — not a solar farm
  that stops at sunset. That is the evening problem, arriving inside a steel plant.
- **Nobody to pass the cost to.** The world has roughly **600 million tonnes** of spare
  steelmaking capacity. Someone will always undercut you with coal-made steel.

**3.6 Cement capture — better odds than coal, and nowhere to put it.**
Here is one place cement is *luckier* than a power station. A coal plant's smoke is about
**one-eighth** carbon dioxide. A cement kiln's is about **one-fifth** — thicker, easier to catch,
and that is why cement, not coal power, is India's realistic capture target. The price says the
same thing: about **65 dollars a tonne** at a cement kiln, against about **75** at a coal plant.
But one-fifth is still mostly ordinary air. Where the waste comes out almost pure, the same tonne
costs about **20 dollars**. Cement still pays three times over. And catching it
is only half the job. India has **no working network to move carbon dioxide and nowhere proven to
store it**. The leading candidate is the ancient volcanic rock under the Deccan, which reacts with
carbon dioxide and turns it to stone so it cannot leak. But almost none of that rock has been
drilled and tested. Nobody has yet confirmed which spots can actually hold carbon, how much, or for
how long.

## 4. Where it actually stands

**4.1 Built and working.**

- **Less clinker in cement — the win India has already taken.** Blended cement is **73%** of
  everything India makes. Carbon per tonne is down from **1.12 tonnes in 1996 to about 0.66**. But
  the room is nearly gone: the government expects demand for coal ash to pass supply between
  **2030 and 2035**.
- **Electric furnaces for steel — the machine is clean, the ingredient is not.** India has
  **117 million tonnes** of electric melting capacity against **88.5 million tonnes** of blast
  furnaces, running every day. But **80%** of India's sponge iron still comes out of coal-fired
  kilns. So an electric furnace gets fed iron that was made with coal.
- **Electric heat below 500 °C — happening, but nobody counts it.** Heat pumps and electric boilers
  are sold off the shelf and mills do buy them. India keeps no register, so there is no number.
  *Our reading is that it is still small. That is our judgement, not a published figure.*

**4.2 Started, not finished.**

**Gas instead of coal to make iron — there is no pipe.** Only **21%** of blast furnace capacity and
**5%** of coal-based sponge iron capacity sits within 25 kilometres of a gas pipeline. For nineteen
plants in twenty, the price of gas never even comes up.

**Hydrogen instead of coal to make iron — four projects, four different stages.**

| Where | What | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| Jindal Stainless, Hisar | **78 tonnes a year** — used in the finishing shop, not for making iron | Running |
| JSW Steel, Vijayanagar | 25 MW plant, **3,600 tonnes a year**, meant for ironmaking | Being built |
| Tata Steel, Jamshedpur | Hydrogen pushed into a blast furnace for **four days** — 10% less coke, **7 to 10% less carbon** | Trial |
| CSIR-IMMT, Bhubaneswar | Iron made with hydrogen alone, no carbon at all | Laboratory |

India has commissioned about **8,000 tonnes a year** of green hydrogen against a national target of
**5 million tonnes**. *That is 0.16% — our arithmetic.*

**Catching carbon at the cement chimney — still on paper, and the government says so.** Its own
roadmaps state there is **no policy for storing carbon, no site drilled and tested, and no pilot
project at all** for moving and storing it. NITI Aayog puts the groundwork alone at **5 to 10
years**. The one plan on the table catches **0.67 million tonnes a year** for ₹1,100 crore, against
a cement industry giving off **246 million tonnes**. Under three tonnes in every thousand.

**4.3 Proof the finished version works.** The two things India has not built are the two already
running abroad. Sweden's HYBRIT made steel with hydrogen instead of coal and sold it to paying
customers in **2021**. Norway's Brevik plant catches the carbon from a cement kiln at full
commercial size. Same chemistry, same machines, already working somewhere else.

## 5. Progress against 2030 and 2070

| | The target | Where India is | How far along |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5.1** | Bring steel's carbon down from **2.54 to 2.20 tonnes** per tonne of steel by 2030 — a 13% cut | Still **2.54** | **0%** — 2.54 is the starting line, and the sector has not stepped off it |
| **5.2** | Get blended cement to **80%** of all cement by 2030 | **73%** | **91%** — the one lever India has almost finished, and almost used up |
| **5.3** | Burn waste instead of coal for **20%** of kiln heat by 2030 | **3–5%** | **~20%** — rich countries are already in double digits |
| **5.4** | **5 million tonnes** of green hydrogen a year by 2030 | about **8,000 tonnes** | **0.16%** — barely begun, and steel is not the buyer yet |
| **5.5** | Catch **0.67 million tonnes** of cement carbon a year | Nothing built | **0%** — no policy, no tested site, no pilot |
| **5.6** | **Net zero by 2070** | **486 million tonnes** a year from steel and cement, still rising | **0%** — the number is moving the wrong way |

*The 2.54 figure is for 2023-24; nothing newer has been published, so steel's row cannot yet show
movement. The 0.16% and the 486 million tonne total are our own arithmetic, and 486 uses the lower
of the two published steel estimates.*

Row 5.4 is not as harsh as it looks for steel alone. The Ministry of Steel thinks steel could
absorb **1.1 million tonnes** of that hydrogen by 2030 — but only if the price falls to about a
dollar a kilo, roughly a quarter of what it costs today.

Read down the last column and the shape of the sector jumps out. **One row is at 91%. Every other
row is at or near zero.** The row that is nearly finished is the cheapest one, the one that needs
no new factory and no new fuel — and it is also the one closest to its ceiling. India has completed
the easy part of industry and has not started the hard part.

### 5.7 The number nobody set a target for — and the only one that matters

The natural thought is: clean up the grid, and the factories clean up with it. India's own Ministry
of Steel says no. Give a steel plant completely clean electricity, every single unit renewable, and
its carbon per tonne falls by **at most 25 to 30%**. The rest does not move, because it never came
from a socket. It came from coal pulling the oxygen out of iron ore. The ministry puts a figure on
the leftover: **about 56% of the carbon in Indian steel can only be removed by catching it at the
chimney** — the one solution with no policy, no tested site and no pilot anywhere in the country.

Cement says the same thing a different way. NITI Aayog's roadmap runs every lever it has to 2070 —
cleaner power, less clinker, waste burnt instead of coal, carbon capture at full scale. Cement
still gives off **198 to 252 million tonnes a year in 2070**, against about **246 million** today.
Fifty years of work, and the total ends up where it began.

Read that correctly, though. India will be making close to **four times as much cement** by then,
so carbon per tonne falls from 0.66 tonnes to **0.09 to 0.13**. That is a real and enormous
achievement. It is also what net zero actually asks of a growing country: not a sector that stops
giving off carbon, but one that gives off the same amount while doing four times the work — and
something else, somewhere else, has to pull the leftover out of the air.

Industry is the easier version of this problem. It has chimneys you can count, a few hundred plants
you can name, and government roadmaps with real numbers in them. India's farms have none of that.
They give off about a fifth of the country's greenhouse gases, from crores of fields and animals
spread across the whole country, and there is no chimney anywhere to fix.

*Steel emission intensity by route, the 2.54 national average and the 2.20 target for 2029-30, the 25-kilometre gas pipeline finding, the share of sponge iron made in coal-fired rotary kilns, the ceiling on what clean electricity alone can do, the 56% that only capture can remove, and the four hydrogen projects all come from the Ministry of Steel's "Greening the Steel Sector in India" roadmap; the clinker factor path, blended cement at 73% and the 80% target, waste-as-fuel at 3 to 5% against 20%, fly-ash supply passing demand by 2030-2035, the 246 million tonne baseline, the 198 to 252 million tonne 2070 outcome, the 0.09 to 0.13 intensity, the ₹1,100 crore capture pilot and the 5-to-10-year lead time from NITI Aayog's "Roadmap for Cement Sector Decarbonisation"; steel output, rank and imported coking coal from the Ministry of Steel and worldsteel; the 56% of cement carbon that comes from limestone, and green hydrogen prices, from the Council on Energy, Environment and Water; scrap use and the scrap shortfall from EY-Parthenon with the Confederation of Indian Industry; grid carbon intensity from the Central Electricity Authority and the gas share of electricity from Ember; the heat-versus-feedstock split, the share of industrial energy needed above 500 °C, the carbon dioxide strength of cement and coal exhaust and the twenty-five physical challenges of the transition from the McKinsey Global Institute; HYBRIT and Brevik from their own operators. Four figures are ours rather than published: the 70 to 90 million tonnes for plastics and fertiliser, built from production times carbon per tonne; the 0.16% green hydrogen progress; the 486 million tonne steel-and-cement total; and the judgement that electrification of low-temperature heat is still small, which India does not measure at all. The three capture costs in 3.6 are read off Exhibit 40 of that study's 2024 full report, not printed as numbers in its text.*

## Working notes — not for the reader

**Structure.** Second sector answer. Follows `12-power.md` exactly and confirms it as the template
for Q14–Q16: five numbered beats with the same five titles word for word — *Where the carbon comes
from · The solutions, ranked · The challenge behind each · Where it actually stands · Progress
against 2030 and 2070*. Beats 2 and 3 carry the same six items in the same order. Beat 4 is the
three-part ladder (built and working / started, not finished / proof the finished version works),
**not** an item-locked six. Beat 5 ends with a "the number nobody set a target for" section.

**The spine.** Heat versus feedstock. Set up in beat 1, it orders beat 2, and it is what beat 5.7
pays off. Everything in this answer hangs off *electricity is not a molecule*.

**The 25 challenges covered here.** Industry's own five are MGI 12–16: furnacing low-emissions
steel · cementing change for construction · cracking the challenge of plastics · synthesising
low-emissions ammonia · heating other industries. This answer covers four of them natively, folds
plastics into 1.3, and borrows two from other domains — *capturing point-source carbon* (2.6/3.6)
and *harnessing hydrogen* (2.5/3.5).

**Denominator discipline.** Steel's ~12% is of India's CO₂; cement's ~7% is of total greenhouse
gases. They are **not added anywhere**. Beat 1 ranks by absolute tonnes and gives percentages only
as asides. Same trap Q12 flagged for 37.5/48/53.

**Numbers deliberately not used.**
- *"Gas fell from X% to 2.3%."* No sourced historical gas **generation** series exists in the
  library. Only the world comparison and the capacity shrinkage are used.
- *"Gas ironmaking is about 50% cleaner."* A secondary source said this; the Ministry of Steel's own
  route table contradicts it. The route figures are used instead.
- *0.71 clinker factor paired with 0.56.* Different bases and years — 0.71 is quoted alone as
  "among the world's lowest", and 0.56 only as the industry's own 2070 plan.
- *The cement roadmap's "1,323 MtCO₂e in 2030".* An obvious typo for 2070; the sentence is not
  quoted.
- *The CCTS "264 MtCO₂e" reduction potential.* Does not reconcile with the stated 3.62%; left out.

**Cement's 2070 number is the honest sting.** 198–252 Mt in 2070 against 246 Mt today, on the
government's own most optimistic run. Not presented as failure — presented as what net zero means
for a country that quadruples its output.

**Open items carried forward.**
- Whether Energy Group has any steel position (needed for Q17; Ambuja and ACC give it the cement position).
- The 8,000 tonnes of commissioned green hydrogen is secondary-sourced, not from either roadmap.
- No India-wide steel intensity figure published after 2023-24, so 5.1 cannot yet show movement.

**Hand-off.** Closes on farming: no chimney, no plant list, no roadmap with numbers — the opposite
