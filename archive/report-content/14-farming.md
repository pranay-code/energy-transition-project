India's largest single climate problem does not come out of a chimney. It comes from the way cattle
digest their food — a slow, natural process happening quietly in tens of crores of animals across
the country. And unlike a power plant or a steel mill, there is no gate to walk through, no
boardroom to convince, and no one machine to replace. There are crores of farmers.

---

## 1. Where the carbon comes from

Start with a correction to the word "carbon". Almost none of farming's pollution is carbon dioxide.
It is two other gases — **methane**, the gas made inside the stomachs of cattle as they digest, and
in flooded fields where the soil runs out of oxygen, and **nitrous oxide**, the gas that rises off
soil when you feed it nitrogen fertiliser. Both trap far more heat than carbon dioxide does. Methane
traps about 21 to 28 times more, tonne for tonne. So a small weight of gas does a large amount of
damage.

**Farming is 19.5 percent of everything India emits** — the third-biggest source, and far above the
world's 12 percent. India's own official books put it lower, at 13.72 percent, or 406 million tonnes
in 2020. Both figures are honest. The gap is mostly about how hard you judge methane — India's books
still use the 1995 multiplier of 21, while newer science uses about 27. Re-run India's own cattle
number at 27 and it jumps from 223 to about 286 million tonnes.

Here is where it comes from. The split is India's own list, and it adds up exactly.

| Source | What it actually is | Tonnes | Of sector | Of India |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cattle digestion** | Methane from the stomachs of cows, buffalo, goats and sheep | 223 Mt | 54.8% | **10.7%** |
| **Fertilised soil** | Nitrous oxide rising off fields after fertiliser goes in | 94 Mt | 23.3% | **4.5%** |
| **Flooded paddy** | Methane from rice fields under standing water | 68 Mt | 16.7% | **3.3%** |
| **Stored dung** | Methane and nitrous oxide from manure in heaps and pits | 13 Mt | 3.2% | **0.6%** |
| **Stubble fires** | Burning what is left in the field after harvest | 8 Mt | 2.0% | **0.4%** |

Two things in that table are worth pausing on. First, **cattle digestion alone is about 10.7 percent
of everything India emits** — India's own inventory ranks it the third-biggest single emission
category in the whole country, behind only electricity generation and road transport. One animal's
digestion out-emits the entire transport sector. Second, the fires that
fill Delhi's air every November are the **smallest** line on the list. They are a serious health
problem. They are a minor climate one.

And farming is not the sector running away. Between 2000 and 2020, India's farming emissions grew
**18 percent**. Its energy emissions grew **132 percent** over the same twenty years. Farming is not
getting worse quickly. It is simply not getting better.

Now the twist. India's cropland covers about half the country's land area, and farmers keep putting
compost and fertiliser into it. In India's own accounts, that soil **absorbs 356 million tonnes of
carbon dioxide a year** — more than double what all of India's forests absorb, and two-thirds of
every tonne the country's land removes from the air. So the same fields that emit 406 million tonnes
also soak up 356 million. You cannot simply subtract one from the other — methane and carbon dioxide
are different gases with different lifetimes, and the soil figure is a model estimate, not a
measurement. But it explains why farming gets talked about differently from steel. In India's books,
the farm is both the problem and the sponge.

## 2. The solutions, ranked

The ranking is easy here, and it is the same ranking as the table above. There is no clever
technology that leapfrogs the list. Each solution attacks one source, so the biggest source has the
biggest prize.

### 2.1 Feed cattle better — 223 million tonnes on the table

Methane from a herd is just two numbers multiplied: how many animals, and how much methane each one
gives off. Most countries can pull the first number down. India cannot — the cattle are protected,
and they are the savings account of poor households. So India's only lever is the second. Better
feed — green fodder, balanced rations, protein that survives the stomach — cuts methane per litre of
milk. And it quietly pulls the first number too, because a cow that gives more milk means fewer cows
are needed for the same milk. This is also where the rest of the world's biggest lever sits and
India's does not: eating less meat. India already eats very little.

### 2.2 Waste less nitrogen fertiliser — 94 million tonnes

Not less food. Less waste. Most nitrogen a farmer spreads never reaches the plant; it escapes as gas
or washes away. Coated urea that releases slowly, the right dose at the right week, soil testing
before you spread — each one cuts the gas without cutting the harvest.

### 2.3 Let the paddy field dry out — 68 million tonnes

Rice makes methane only because standing water shuts oxygen out of the soil. Let the field dry once
or twice during the season and the methane collapses. Rice yield does not. But the prize is smaller
than it first looks, and India's own survey says why: of India's 45.24 million hectares of rice,
only **22.3 percent is continuously flooded**. Another **39 percent already dries out several
times** a season. The easy part is largely done.

### 2.4 Seal dung in a tank instead of leaving it in a heap — 13 million tonnes

Seal manure in a tank instead of leaving it in the open, and the methane it would have released
becomes cooking gas or vehicle fuel, with fertiliser left over. This is the only solution on the
list that **earns twice** — it removes a farm emission and replaces a fossil fuel at the same time.
That is why it punches above its 3 percent.

### 2.5 Clear stubble without setting fire to it — 8 million tonnes

Last by carbon, and it will always be last by carbon. But the same residue, collected instead of
burnt, is the feedstock that makes 2.4 work at scale.

## 3. The challenge behind each

Before the list, one honest note. The standard list of twenty-five physical challenges[^fw] of the energy transition contains **no farming challenge at all**. Not one. There is a slot for aviation fuel and a
slot for critical minerals; there is none for a cow. That is not an oversight — those twenty-five
are challenges of the *energy* system, and farming's gases are biological, not energy. Four of the
twenty-five brush past the edges: clean ammonia (which is how fertiliser is made, not how it is
spread), sustainable biofuel feedstock, carbon removal, and cutting demand. None of them reaches
inside the field. **So the five challenges below are ours, not from that list.** They match the five
solutions one for one, and each one names a *different* missing thing — which is why the list is
complete rather than just long.

### 3.1 The better feed does not exist yet

This is a shortage of land, not of willingness. Green fodder has to be grown, and it competes with
food crops for the same soil and the same water. You cannot feed 30 crore cattle and buffalo on
something India does not grow enough of. *The missing thing is material.*

### 3.2 The price tells the farmer to use more

Urea in India sells far below what it costs to make. When a bag is cheap, spreading an extra one is
a rational thing to do. India's own inventory shows the result plainly: nitrogen use rose from 16.96
to 20.08 million tonnes in three years, and soil emissions jumped **6.82 percent in a single year** —
a rise the government attributes, in its own words, to synthetic fertiliser. *The missing thing is a
price that points the other way.*

### 3.3 You cannot drain a field you cannot fill

Letting a paddy dry and re-flooding it on purpose requires the farmer to decide *when* water
arrives. Most Indian paddy runs on a canal schedule set by someone else, or on the monsoon. The
field also has to be level, or half of it floods while half of it cracks. *The missing thing is
water control — which is concrete and pipe, not advice.*

### 3.4 The dung is not in one place

The digester is the easy part; India has been building them for forty years. The wall is collection.
Dung has to be gathered every single day from crores of scattered sheds, and a large share of it is
dropped in the open while the animal grazes and is never collected at all. *The missing thing is
logistics.*

### 3.5 The gap is two to three weeks, not a season

Between cutting paddy and sowing wheat, a Punjab farmer has barely a fortnight. Burning the field
clears it in an afternoon and costs nothing. A machine that chops the residue into the soil instead
takes days, costs money, and needs a tractor strong enough to pull it. The farmer is not choosing
pollution over cleanliness. He is choosing the wheat crop. *The missing thing is time.*

Behind all five sits the wall that makes farming different from every other sector. Steel is a few
hundred plants. Cement is a few hundred kilns. Farming is crores of separate people, each making
their own decision every season, most of them with no cushion if the decision goes wrong.

## 4. Where it actually stands

India's Fourth Biennial Update Report lists, scheme by scheme, exactly how much each farm programme
cut between 2019 and 2024. It is one of the most useful tables the government publishes, and it
tells a very sharp story.

### 4.1 Built and working

One thing on the whole list has worked at national scale. Since 2016, **every bag of urea made in
India must be neem-coated** — the coating slows the release so more nitrogen reaches the plant and
less escapes as gas. Saving over five years: **26.81 million tonnes**. And look at *why* it worked.
No farmer had to learn anything, buy anything, or change anything. The change was made once, at the
factory, and it arrived in every field in the country automatically.

### 4.2 Started, but not finished

Everything that needs the farmer to act personally is still a pilot. Over the same five years:

- **Rice**, all three programmes together — the System of Rice Intensification across 193 districts,
  direct-seeded rice, and crop diversification: **0.40 million tonnes**.
- **Stubble**, through crop-residue machinery: **1.45 million tonnes**.
- **Cattle feed**, all four programmes together — balanced rations, bypass protein, green fodder and
  silage: **1.85 million tonnes**, reaching about **45 lakh animals** out of a herd of more than
  30 crore. That is roughly one and a half animals in every hundred.
- **Farm energy** — solar pumps under PM-KUSUM and farm demand-side management:
  **1.48 million tonnes**.
- **Dung to gas** — 89 biogas and compressed-biogas plants nationally as of October 2024, with 198
  more added in 2023-24. No emission saving is even reported for them yet.

### 4.3 Proof the unfinished part can finish

Two pieces of evidence, both from inside India. The **Rashtriya Gokul Mission** breeds indigenous
cattle for higher milk yield, and India credits it with **2.09 million tonnes** — the largest
livestock saving on the list, and proof that the productivity route in 2.1 genuinely works, because
more milk per animal really does mean less methane per litre. And the compressed-biogas plants that
do exist produce **4.92 lakh kilograms of gas a day**. The machine is not the problem. The number of
them is.

Put the whole list together and the total is **34.1 million tonnes over five years**. Neem-coated
urea alone is **79 percent** of it. Every other farm programme India runs — every pilot, every
subsidy, every district demonstration, every animal fed, combined — is the remaining 21 percent.
Four out of every five tonnes India has cut in farming came from a single decision taken inside a
fertiliser factory, and not one of them required a farmer to do anything differently.

## 5. Progress against 2030 and 2070

The table below re-sorts the same five solutions — not by size any more, but by how fast each one is
actually moving. The order flips almost completely.

| Solution | Prize each year | Cut in five years | Speed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.2 Waste less nitrogen fertiliser | 94 Mt | 26.8 Mt | **5.7% a year** | The only real win |
| 2.5 Clear stubble without burning it | 8 Mt | 1.4 Mt | **3.6% a year** | Machines out, fires still lit |
| 2.1 Feed cattle better | 223 Mt | 3.9 Mt | **0.4% a year** | Biggest prize, slowest lever |
| 2.3 Let the paddy field dry out | 68 Mt | 0.4 Mt | **0.1% a year** | Real pilots, no scale |
| 2.4 Seal dung in a tank | 13 Mt | not counted | **near zero** | Proven machine, 89 of them |
| **Whole sector** | **406 Mt** | **34.1 Mt** | **1.7% a year** | Falling behind |

*The 34.1 Mt total also carries 1.5 Mt from solar pumps and farm electricity savings, which India's
inventory counts under energy rather than farming, so it sits in no row above.*

Now the reasoning behind each line, because the percentage on its own hides the story.

**Nitrogen is the only real win, and it shows both the power and the limit of the method.** It
worked because it never asked a farmer to do anything — the coating was added at the factory and
arrived in every field automatically. But coating only slows nitrogen from escaping. It does not
stop over-use. While the coating spread across the whole country, nitrogen consumption itself still
climbed from 16.96 to 20.08 million tonnes. India is losing a smaller share of a bigger pile.

**Stubble moves second-fastest and matters least in carbon terms.** The machines exist and are
subsidised, so this was never a technology gap. What has not changed is the two-to-three week window
between cutting paddy and sowing wheat. A farmer short of time still burns, because burning takes an
afternoon and the machine takes days.

**Cattle feed is the biggest prize and the slowest lever, by a wide margin.** About 45 lakh animals
were reached in five years, out of a herd of more than 30 crore. At that pace it would take over
three centuries just to touch the herd once. The block is not knowledge — every one of these
programmes works, and the breeding programme alone delivered the largest livestock saving on the
list. It is that India does not grow enough green fodder to feed the animals it already has.

**Paddy has genuine reach and no scale.** Programmes are running in 193 districts across 25 states,
which is real. But drying a field on purpose needs the farmer to decide when water arrives, and most
Indian paddy runs on a canal schedule set by someone else. These pilots are limited by pipes, not by
persuasion.

**Dung is the only line where India does not yet report a saving at all.** Nothing here is
experimental — the plants that exist already make 4.92 lakh kilograms of gas a day. There are simply
89 of them, for a country with crores of cattle sheds. The missing piece is collection, not
conversion.

**And the sector as a whole is falling behind.** At 6.8 million tonnes a year, it would take roughly
**60 years of savings to equal a single year of emissions**. Worse, avoided is not the same as
reduced. The schemes report savings every year, and the national inventory still shows farming
running 18 percent above its year-2000 level. Both are true, because a scheme counts what *would*
have been emitted without it, while the inventory counts what actually was.

### 5.1 The number nobody set a target for — and the only one that matters

For every other sector, this section compares progress against a target. For farming, it cannot —
because there is no target to compare against.

India's climate pledge to the United Nations promises to cut the emissions intensity of its economy
by **45 percent by 2030** against 2005 levels, and India is **80 percent of the way there** already,
having reached 36 percent by 2020. But that pledge sets no targets by sector, and on farming India
is explicit. Its own report to the UN states, in one flat sentence: **"India's voluntary declaration
excludes mitigation in the agricultural sector."** India has also chosen not to join the Global
Methane Pledge, and has said why — the pledge would fall on small and marginal farmers, and food
comes first.

So the honest score for farming against 2030 and 2070 is not a low percentage. It is a blank.

Which leaves one number standing alone. **223 million tonnes of methane from cattle digestion** —
the third-largest single source of emissions in India, growing slowly, deliberately left out of the
national pledge, and with no plan attached to it. Every other number in this sector is small enough
to argue about. That one is not.

And notice what the one success actually proves. The neem-coated urea worked because it was
installed **upstream** — in a factory, in a pipe, in a piece of infrastructure that crores of people
already touch without choosing to. That is the only route in farming that has ever moved at national
speed. It is also the reason the next sector matters so much. Because the moment a farmer buys an
electric pump instead of a diesel one, or a household plugs in instead of filling a tank, the
decision stops being theirs and starts being the grid's. And India's grid is about to be asked to
carry the entire country's transport as well.

the 6.82 percent one-year rise in soil emissions and the nitrogen consumption trend, the
356 MtCO<sub>2</sub>e cropland sink, the scheme-by-scheme mitigation table for 2019–24, the NDC intensity
progress, and the sentence excluding agriculture from India's mitigation declaration. Livestock herd
