India has already won the easy half of this. It has built more clean power machines than dirty
ones — and it still gets three-quarters of its electricity from coal. Both sentences are true at
the same time, and the gap between them is the whole story.

## 1. Where the carbon comes from

**Making electricity is 37.5 percent of everything India emits** — the single biggest source in the
country, and nearly twice the next one. Count only the carbon India makes by *burning fuel*, setting
cattle and paddy fields aside, and power's share rises to 53 percent, against 46 percent worldwide.
Both numbers are right; they simply measure against different totals.

| | Machines built | Electricity made | Of the sector's carbon | Of India |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1.1 Coal and lignite** | 42.1% | 70.8% | **~98%** | **~36.8%** |
| **1.2 Gas and diesel** | 3.8% | 2.5% | ~2% | ~0.7% |
| **1.3 Everything clean** | 54.2% | 26.6% | ~0% | ~0% |

Look at the last column. **Coal on its own is about 36.8 percent of India's carbon** — more than
industry, farming and transport can each claim, and more than transport and farming combined.

Read row 1.1 against row 1.3 and the whole problem appears. Coal owns two-fifths of the machines
and makes seven-tenths of the power. Clean owns more than half the machines and makes one-quarter
of it.

Why? **A coal plant is a tap. A solar farm is rain.** You open a tap when you want water. Rain
comes when it comes. In India the sun shines for about a fifth of the hours in a year, so half the
machines sit idle most of the time while coal keeps running.

Two more leaks sit on top of the burning.

- **1.4 One unit in six never reaches anyone.** Losses across transmission and distribution are
  19.27%. Picture six truckloads of coal going into a power station — one truckload's worth of
  electricity disappears in the wires before it reaches a single home.
- **1.5 Demand will not sit still.** India's peak hit **270,832 megawatts** in May 2026, up 15% in
  a single year.

So the sector's carbon is one number in one place: **about 124 crore tonnes a year, almost all of
it from coal, and up 36% in five years.**

## 2. The solutions, ranked

Ranked by one test: **how many tonnes of coal burning it removes per year, at today's price and
today's build time.** Build time counts as much as price. A solution that arrives in 2035 removes
nothing in 2030.

- **2.1 Build more solar and wind.** Cheapest power in India, and a solar farm goes up in months.
  India added a record 54.6 gigawatts in one year. The engine already works.
- **2.2 Put batteries next to them.** A battery makes no power of its own. It is a bucket. It
  catches sunshine at noon, when nobody wants it, and pours it out at 10 pm, when everybody does.
  Build time is roughly a year to eighteen months — and Energy Group built 3.37 gigawatt-hours at Khavda
  in about ten.
- **2.3 Build the wires.** This removes carbon without making a single new unit. It only stops
  clean power that already exists from being thrown away.
- **2.4 Pumped hydro and nuclear.** Big, firm, clean, slow. Pumped hydro takes **4 to 6 years**.
  Nuclear takes over 10.
- **2.5 Cut the losses, and move demand into the daytime.** No new power plant at all. Fix the
  leaking wires. And get the big users — factories, and the pumps farmers use to water their
  fields — to run in the afternoon when solar is spare, instead of in the evening when only coal
  is left.
- **2.6 Trap the carbon at the chimney.** Let the coal plant burn, but catch the carbon dioxide
  before it leaves the stack and bury it underground. Last on the list, and by a distance — India
  has essentially none of this running, and as 3.6 explains, a coal chimney is the hardest place on
  earth to do it.

> **A quick note on two words that look alike.** A **gigawatt** is a *size* — how much power
> something can push out at this instant. A **gigawatt-hour** is an *amount* — how much
> electricity that adds up to over time. A tap has a width; a bucket has a volume. For batteries
> you always need both numbers.

**Why 2.2 beats simply building more of 2.1.** India's midday is already full. Extra solar made at
noon has no buyer, so it is switched off and lost — that is happening today. A battery does not add
to the midday flood; it takes water out of it and delivers it at 10 pm, and the only other seller
at 10 pm is a coal plant. **The same sunshine, stored, pushes coal off the grid. Unstored, it does
not.**

## 3. The challenge behind each

Same six, same numbers, so you can read 2.1 against 3.1. These are physical reasons, not money
reasons.

### 3.1 Solar and wind — the sun sets before the peak

On 24 May 2026 India's demand peaked at **22:36**. Solar output at 22:36 is zero.

{{exhibit:ex-day-curve}}


Three separate physical problems live in that one picture.

- **The evening ramp.** India must switch on about **60 gigawatts** in a few hours every evening,
  sometimes at **500 megawatts every minute** — the same as firing up a large power station every
  two minutes, every evening, forever.
- **The grid gets jumpy.** A coal plant spins a heavy metal shaft, and that spinning weight
  steadies the grid by itself, the way a heavy flywheel keeps a machine smooth. A solar panel has
  no moving parts at all. Swap enough turbines for panels and the steadiness goes. India's grid
  frequency, which should sit at a rock-steady 50, swung between 49.65 and 50.35 on one measured
  day.
- **Land.** A gigawatt of solar needs roughly **2,000 hectares** — about 20 square kilometres, with
  a clean title, for every single gigawatt.

### 3.2 Batteries — India has 1.4% of what it needs

**5.9 gigawatt-hours built, against 411 needed by 2031-32.** That is one and a half kilometres of
a hundred-kilometre road.

Three physical problems sit inside the battery itself.

- **You lose a tenth of it.** Put ten units in, get about nine back. The tenth leaves as heat.
- **Heat ages it, and India is hot.** Inside one storage container the bottom shelf runs at
  23–24 °C while the top packs run above 32 °C — the top floor of a building in May. The top packs
  wear out first.
- **We import the expensive part.** The cell alone is **70% of the whole system's cost**, and India
  mines almost no lithium, cobalt or nickel.

### 3.3 Wires — clean power is being thrown away right now

**One-third of India's newly built renewable capacity is running on a temporary grid connection.**
Rajasthan holds 27% of India's solar and has been forced to curtail **up to 80%** of it at times —
four units out of every five the panels made, switched off and lost, because there was no wire to
carry them away.

So why not just build the wire? Because a solar farm sits on **one** piece of land, bought once. A
transmission line has to cross **hundreds** of pieces of land in a row, and every single owner must
agree. One farmer refusing stops the whole line. That is why panels take months and lines take
years.

### 3.4 Pumped hydro and nuclear — they cannot arrive in time

Nothing is wrong with either. The clock is wrong. Start a pumped-hydro scheme today at four to six
years and it lands after 2030; nuclear lands in the late 2030s. These are answers for 2040 — which
is exactly why they have to be started now.

### 3.5 Losses — this is a construction problem, not a billing problem

Cutting losses is the cheapest tonne of carbon in the whole system, and it needs no new technology
at all. But most of that 19.27% is not theft. **It is physics.** Electricity turns into heat as it
travels, and it turns into far more heat in a thin wire, a long wire, or an overloaded one. India's
last mile is all three — long low-voltage lines running from an overloaded local transformer to a
whole colony.

The fix is therefore steel and copper, not software: more transformers, placed closer to people;
thicker conductors; and carrying the high voltage further before stepping it down. Theft and unpaid
bills are real and they add to the total, but the wire itself is the bigger job — and the one that
needs money rather than enforcement.

### 3.6 Carbon capture — the coal chimney is the worst case there is

The idea sounds like the perfect shortcut: keep the plant, catch the smoke. The physics disagrees.

**Coal smoke is only about one-eighth carbon dioxide.** The rest is mostly ordinary air. So the
machine has to take in eight times more gas than the carbon it actually catches. **It is picking
twelve red marbles out of a jar of a hundred — you still have to handle all hundred.**

Now compare a gas-processing plant, where the waste stream is **almost pure carbon dioxide**. There
is nothing to sort. The cost follows exactly: about **20 dollars a tonne** where the stream is pure,
against about **75 dollars** at a coal plant — nearly four times, for the same tonne of carbon. And
the capture machinery eats a slice of the plant's own electricity on top.

Coal power is among the most dilute sources there is, which makes it one of the last places this
technology will become cheap.

> **The honest one.** Alongside all of this, India plans roughly **97,000 megawatts of new coal**.
> India is not replacing its coal fleet. It is stacking clean capacity on top of a coal fleet that
> is still growing, because demand grows faster than either.

## 4. Where it actually stands

**4.1 Built and working.**

- **297,369 megawatts** of non-fossil capacity — 54.18% of the total, four years ahead of the
  pledge.
- **509,603 circuit kilometres** of high-voltage line.
- Over five years, **134 gigawatts of renewables added against 21 gigawatts of net new coal**.
- And in 2025 coal generation actually **fell** — from 1,518 to 1,474 billion units. A unit is what
  your electricity bill counts. It is the first real fall outside covid.

**4.2 Started, not finished.** Batteries stopped being a pilot in 2026.

| Project | Size | Live |
|---|---|---|
| Energy Group Renewables, Khavda, Gujarat | **3.37 gigawatt-hours** | Mar 2026 |
| IndiGrid, Gujarat | 360 megawatt-hours | Feb 2026 |
| IndiGrid, Rajasthan | 500 megawatt-hours | Jun 2026 |
| Uttar Pradesh (two projects) | 1,250 megawatt-hours | early 2027 |

Look at the first row against India's national total of 5.9 gigawatt-hours. **More than half of
every battery on India's grid sits at one site in Kutch** — and that site is contracted to reach 14
gigawatt-hours, which alone is more than twice everything India has today.

Costs have fallen about **80% in a decade**, and the money and the rules are already sitting there:
**₹18,100 crore** of manufacturing incentives, **40%** viability gap funding, a rule requiring 4%
of power to come through storage by 2030, and permission for a developer to sell stored sunshine in
non-solar hours.

**4.3 Proof the finished version works.** On 29 March 2026 at 7 pm, batteries on California's grid
supplied **12.29 gigawatts — 42.8% of everything that grid was using at that moment.** Nearly half
a large grid's evening, running on stored sunshine. That is India's 22:36 problem, already solved
somewhere else, at scale, with the same chemistry.

### 5.6 The number nobody set a target for — and the only one that matters

Row 5.3 asks *how many units were clean*. This asks something different: **how much carbon is
inside one average unit of electricity** — the number that decides what a factory, a train or an
electric scooter is really plugging into.

India's answer was 0.703 kilograms of carbon dioxide per unit in 2020-21. Five years later, after
the fastest clean build in the country's history, it is **0.712**. It went slightly up.

Here is why, and it is not because demand grew.

**Picture a bucket of muddy water.** You pour in clean water, so the bucket should get clearer.
India poured in a lot — renewable generation grew 73% in those five years. By our own arithmetic on
the official figures, that alone should have pulled the average down by about **4%**.

It went **up 1%** instead. The whole five-point difference is the muddy water in the bucket getting
muddier.

Two things did that. India's gas plants make **less than half** the carbon of a coal plant for the
same unit — and imported gas became so expensive that gas shrank to just 2.3% of India's
electricity, with coal filling the hole. At the same time coal plants were pushed harder: their
utilisation jumped from 66.6% to 72.4% in a single year.

**So the clean water going in was almost exactly cancelled by the rest of the bucket getting
dirtier.** The renewables were completely real. They just were not allowed to displace anything.

That is the whole answer in one line. India is not behind on building. It is behind on **timing** —
on the four hours between six and ten at night, when the sun is gone and coal is the only seller
left. That is a battery and wires problem, and both are finally moving.

Electricity is the gate almost everything else waits at. But factories cannot afford to wait for
it. And a steel plant has a harder problem than a slow grid: even with perfectly clean power in the
socket, some of its carbon never came from electricity at all.
