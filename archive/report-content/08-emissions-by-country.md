Let's look at the scoreboard. Three countries do most of the emitting. China is far in front, putting
out roughly 30% of the world's carbon dioxide. The United States is next at about 13%. India comes
third — but at only around 7–8% of the total, it's a distant third, well behind the top two.

But totals only tell half the story. India also has a huge population, so if we share the emissions
out per person, the picture flips completely. An average Indian gives off about 2 tonnes of carbon
dioxide a year — less than half the world average of roughly 4.7 tonnes, and a small fraction of an
American's. Over all of history, too, India has added only about 3–4% of the carbon humans have ever
put in the air, though it is home to one in six people on Earth.

But here's the catch. India is growing fast, and its people rightly want better lives — more
electricity, travel and comfort. Just imagine every Indian used energy like the world average person:
India's yearly emissions would more than double, from around 3 to nearly 7 billion tonnes. So the
world's climate future depends heavily on the path India takes. Which is exactly why India's own
targets matter — and "net zero" is a fuzzy phrase, so what are the hard, objective numbers India is
actually aiming for?

*Sources: Global Carbon Project, 2024 (national and per-capita CO<sub>2</sub>, cumulative shares); UN World Population Prospects, 2023. The world-average scenario is our own illustrative calculation — 1.4 billion people × 4.7 tonnes — not a forecast.*
