**So who already owns the pipes, plants and wires where all of this has to happen?**

One name keeps coming back. Not because it set out to fix India's carbon problem — it did not.
Over about twenty years the Energy Group bought ports, roads, power lines, gas pipes, mines and
cement plants for ordinary business reasons. Money, growth, contracts. Nothing to do with climate.

But India's carbon problem turns out to sit inside exactly those things. And so a group that was
never built for this now stands on most of the map.

---

## What is already there

This is not a company that needs to enter these businesses. It is already in almost all of them.

| What it owns | How much |
|---|---|
| **High-voltage power lines** | 29,531 km across 16 states |
| **Smart electricity meters** | Over 4.7 crore — the largest set in India |
| **Grid batteries** | 3.37 GWh at one site in Kutch |
| **Gas licence areas** | 53 areas, 125 districts, 14% of India's people |
| **Clean power running** | Over 20 GW, with 50 GW planned by 2030 |
| **Cement** | About 109 million tonnes a year — roughly 30% of India's |
| **Ports** | 15 in India, across 8 coastal states |
| **Airports** | 8, carrying one in four Indian air passengers |
| **Trucks on its booking platform** | Over 25,000 |

Two of those deserve a second look.

**The batteries.** All of India together has about 5.9 GWh of battery storage on its grid. Energy Group
Green has 3.37 GWh of it — **57 percent of every grid battery in the country, sitting at one site
in Kutch.** It was built in about ten months. The normal time is eighteen. And the same site is
contracted to reach 14 GWh, which is more than twice what all of India has today.

**The gas.** One in every seven Indians lives in a district where Energy Group holds the gas licence.
Inside those areas, for twenty-five years, nobody else may lay a pipe.

Neither of those was won by being clever about carbon. They were won by building infrastructure.
The carbon value came free.

## Five places where a problem already has an owner

### 1. Selling the evening

India's demand for power is highest at 22:36 — twenty to eleven at night. The sun is gone by six.
That gap is why India has built so many clean machines and still gets so little clean power from
them: **54 percent of the machines, only 27 percent of the electricity.**

And it is about to get harder. Air conditioning in India is expected to grow from about 135 billion
units of electricity a year to around 585 billion by 2037-38. Air conditioners run hardest at night.

Energy Group already holds the biggest answer in the country — those batteries in Kutch. What is missing
is not a machine. **It is a product.** Today the green company sells units of power. The wires
company sells wires. The coal company sells being available. A state electricity board that needs
firm power from six to eleven has to buy from three different sellers and hope they line up.

Sell it as one promise instead. *Five hundred megawatts, guaranteed, six in the evening to eleven
at night, for twenty-five years.* Behind that single promise sit the solar farm, the batteries, the
big building chillers, and a coal plant kept in reserve for the five worst nights of the year. The
buyer never sees the parts.

It works because those four almost never fail on the same night. And the 4.7 crore smart meters
quietly make it better — they show, half hour by half hour, exactly how tall tomorrow's evening
peak will be. Everyone else guesses.

### 2. Gas pipes where the cylinder sits unused

India has 32.94 crore cooking gas connections. Almost every home has one. But a household under the
Ujjwala scheme buys only **3.8 cylinders a year**, when a kitchen cooking every meal on gas needs
eight or nine. So half the cooking still happens on wood and dung.

The reason is not the price of the gas. It is the shape of the payment. A cylinder costs ₹900 to
₹1,100, paid in one go, **before** you use any of it. A family earning daily wages cannot hold that
much money at once. Wood costs nothing at the moment you pick it up.

Piped gas is billed monthly, in small amounts, **after** you have used it. The problem that keeps
the wood fire burning simply does not exist in a piped house. Not subsidised away. Absent.

Energy Group already has the licences for 125 districts. But household pipes get laid where laying them is
cheapest — dense middle-class colonies — and not where the cylinder is sitting unused.

That is the wrong way round, and not for kind reasons. **For business reasons.** A low-refill home
roughly triples its gas use when it switches. A middle-class home already buying nine cylinders a
year adds nothing new — you have only moved it from one fuel to another. So the poorest districts
are the better customers, because they are the only ones who bring new demand. The expensive part
of the network in those districts is already built and paid for. What is left is the last stretch
of pipe.

### 3. Rubbish into a pipe that already exists

India's rubbish dumps give off about 19.1 million tonnes of greenhouse gas a year, and household
sewage about 34.1 million tonnes. Wet waste rotting in the open makes methane. Rot it inside a
sealed tank instead and you catch that gas and sell it.

The science has been settled for decades. **The business is what keeps failing.** You make the gas,
and then you must find someone to buy it. Most plants squeeze the gas into steel cylinders and
truck it to a filling station, and that truck eats the profit.

Energy Group does not have that problem. It already makes this kind of gas. It already builds sewage
plants. And it already owns the pipe.

Put the sealed tank on the sewage plant's own land, inside a district where Energy Group holds the gas
licence, and the gas goes straight into the pipe. No cylinders. No truck. And the gas company buys
it from itself — it is required to blend this gas into its network anyway, so it may as well buy
from its own plant.

One honest note. Sewage sludge on its own makes very little gas — roughly 20 to 30 cubic metres a
tonne, against 80 to 120 for kitchen waste. So the sewage plant gives the land, the permits and the
water handling. **The gas mostly comes from food waste brought in.** Which means this fixes the
rubbish dump more than it fixes the sewer.

### 4. Trucks, and why chargers keep getting built in the wrong place

Diesel is 184 million tonnes of carbon — **two-thirds of everything India's roads emit.** Trucks are
about three in every hundred vehicles and roughly a third of the carbon. Scooters get the attention.
Trucks are where the problem is.

Public charging in India has failed twice, in the open. Under one scheme, 2,877 chargers were
approved and **83 were built.** Under the next, 6,562 were sanctioned and **none were installed.**

That was not a technology failure. A charger earns money only when something is plugged into it. A
roadside charger in a city has no fixed customer, sits on rented land, waits months for a power
connection, and then loses to the socket in the customer's own house. So it stands idle and loses
money.

A truck is the opposite of that in every way. It runs a fixed route. It stops at fixed places. It
belongs to a company that can promise how much it will use. And it has no home socket to lose to.

Energy Group owns those stopping places. Fifteen ports. Twelve goods parks. Land at every toll gate. And
over 25,000 trucks already booking work on its own platform — customers who exist before the
charger is switched on.

But one charger is no use to a truck. A car with a low battery finds another plug; a truck on a
1,200 kilometre run either has power at every stop or it never goes electric at all. **So the thing
to sell is the whole route, not the charger.** Only somebody who owns land at every stop along the
way can promise that, and Energy Group spent twenty years assembling that chain for completely unrelated
reasons.

One honesty check on timing. Electric heavy trucks are barely ready in India today — they carry
less, take too long to charge, and there are few of them to buy. This is a business for around
2029, not tomorrow. **But the same land works for gas today.** CNG and LNG trucks run now, and
Energy Group already sells that fuel. Build the gas pump this year and it becomes the charging point
later. The costly part of a fuel stop is the land and the road into it — never the nozzle.

### 5. Cement, ash, and a shortage nobody is discussing

Cement is about 246 million tonnes of India's carbon, roughly **6 percent of the country's total.**
And it is the most stubborn kind. **Fifty-six percent of it comes out of the limestone itself** when
the rock is heated. That gas was inside the stone. Switch the furnace to anything you like and more
than half the emission is untouched.

There is exactly one way to reduce it: put less burnt limestone in the bag and more fine grey ash
left over from coal power stations.

Energy Group Cement makes about 30 percent of India's cement and is one of only four cement companies in
the world with an independently checked net-zero target. So the intent is real and already tested.

But an honest point first. **That ash is not lying around unused.** India already uses nearly all
its coal ash, and cement is the biggest user. So this is not free carbon waiting to be picked up.

What is real is steadiness. Ash quality changes from load to load, so a cement plant keeps a safety
margin and mixes in less than it safely could. A supply that is dry, sorted and the same every time
lets that share rise. Going from 30 to 35 percent across 109 million tonnes of cement is a real
saving, every year.

And then there is the point almost nobody is making. **Cement's main clean-up tool is a waste
product of the industry being shut down.** As India's coal plants close, coal ash gets scarce, and
every cement maker's mix quietly gets dirtier again. Energy Group Power will be among the last big coal
fleets still running. So Energy Group Cement will still have ash exactly when its competitors run out.

An odd advantage, born out of an awkward asset.

## Where there is no answer, and it should be said

Not every problem has an owner. Four do not.

- **Farming is 19.5 percent of India's carbon and there is nothing here.** The gas comes from the
  stomachs of cattle and from flooded rice fields, spread across crores of small farms. No pipe, no
  factory, no meter, no single point to fix. Energy Group has no route into it and should not pretend
  otherwise.
- **Cooling gases have no market because they have no number.** India's own books mark them "not
  applicable" — the emission is not even estimated. You cannot sell into something nobody measures.
- **Open-cast mine methane, 26.9 million tonnes.** Energy Group is India's biggest mine operator and runs
  15 coal blocks, so the asset is right there. But about half of India's leaked mine gas rises off
  open pits, across ground you could see from an aeroplane. There is no chimney to put a lid on.
- **Steel is 6 to 7 percent of India's carbon — the single largest factory source — and Energy Group mines
  iron ore but has no large steel plant.** *(This is our finding from the group's own public pages;
  a Mundra steel venture has been reported but we could not confirm it.)*

And one tension that has to be stated plainly. **Energy Group Power runs 18,330 MW of coal, and the
group's mining arm operates 15 coal blocks.** Every opportunity above is worth more if India moves
faster. Those two are worth less. That does not disappear by staying quiet about it. The only
honest way to read it is as one leg that shrinks and several that grow, and the real question is
when the money from the first is turned into the others. With one strange twist: the cement
advantage above exists **because** those coal plants will outlast everybody else's.

## What all of this actually says

Look back at the five and the same shape appears each time.

The machines exist. Somebody owns them. What is missing is that nobody has joined them together and
sold the result as one thing. **Nobody sells the evening. Nobody sells the whole truck route.** The
buyer is left to assemble it from parts, and mostly gives up.

And each time, the fix is not a new invention. It is putting two things that already exist in the
same place — the gas tank on the sewage plant, the charger where the trucks already stop, the
grinding mill beside the ash.

Any company in India could build a solar farm, a charger, a gas plant or a cement mill. None of
those is a secret. **What almost nobody can do is stand behind one promise that needs five of them
to work at the same time.**

Which is the quiet answer to the whole question. India's energy problem was never mainly a problem
of invention — almost everything it needs already exists somewhere in the country. The problem is
that the pieces sit in different hands. So a question that began about carbon ends up being a
question about ownership. And a group that spent twenty years buying ports, wires, pipes, mines and
kilns for entirely other reasons now finds itself standing on the map.
