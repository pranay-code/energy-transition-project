# Chapter 1: The Beginning
*Realising the problem and understanding how we got here.*

### Q1: What is climate, is it the same thing as weather?

Let's play a game. If you look out your window right now, what do you see? Maybe it is pouring rain in Mumbai, or maybe the sun is baking the streets of Delhi. That is weather. It is what the air is doing right this second. It is chaotic, it is noisy, and it changes by the hour. 

Now, let’s step back. Imagine taking a photo of that exact same window every single day for thirty years, and then blending all those photos together. What you get is a blur, but it is a very specific blur! You will notice a pattern. You will see that Mumbai always gets its rains in June, but almost never in January. That pattern, that long-term average, is climate. 

So, weather is the daily roll of the dice. Climate is how the dice are loaded. 

But what happens when those dice start rolling numbers we have never seen before?

*Sources: IPCC AR6 WG1, 2025.*

### Q2: Okay, so what is this "climate change" everyone is talking about?

Climate change simply means that our long-term pattern has started to shift. It is not about a single hot afternoon or a sudden rainstorm. It means the normal itself is shifting. It means summers that grow hotter and hotter, decade after decade, or a monsoon that slowly starts arriving later than it used to.

But here is the real puzzle. The climate of our Earth has actually shifted many times before. Great ice ages came and went, but they always moved at a slow, lazy crawl. When the last ice age ended, it took our Earth about 1,700 years to warm up by just 1 degree Celsius. 

But look at what we have done lately. We have forced our giant home to warm up by that same 1 degree in just forty years! 

That is not a crawl. That is a sprint. We are turning up the heat on our giant home at a speed that has never happened in history. Which leaves us with a very simple, urgent question: is this rapid change actually bad for us?

*Sources: IPCC AR6 WG1 (palaeoclimate warming rates, deglacial period); HadCRUT5 and NOAA GML (recent global temperature), 2025.*

### Q3: Rapid climate change, is this bad? And why?

Yes, it is very bad, and the real culprit is the speed. 

You see, if things change slowly over thousands of years, nature is incredibly smart. Plants, animals, and even humans have plenty of time to slowly adjust and adapt to the world around them. That is what life on Earth has done for millions of years. But that kind of natural adaptation needs thousands of years, not forty! When you turn up the heat this fast, nature and people simply cannot keep up. 

Because the shift is so fast, our normal weather is starting to break. We are suddenly seeing terrible heatwaves, rains that cause floods in a single afternoon, and long droughts where crops die. 

To make things worse, this heat is starting to trigger some dangerous loops. Think about the white ice at the North and South Poles. Ice acts like a giant mirror, bouncing the sun's heat back into space. But as our giant home warms, that ice melts and uncovers the dark ocean water underneath. 

Now, what happens when you wear a dark shirt on a sunny day? It soaks up the heat, right? The dark ocean water does the exact same thing. It soaks up the sun's heat, which warms the water further, which melts even more ice, which uncovers even more dark water. 

Once a loop like this starts spinning, it is incredibly hard to stop, and it pushes our giant home further and further toward real damage. So, the change is fast, it is breaking our weather, and it is speeding up on its own. 

Which leaves us with a great detective mystery: who started it?

*Sources: IPCC AR6 WG2 (limits to adaptation, extreme weather impacts); IPCC AR6 WG1 SPM and SR1.5 (self-reinforcing feedbacks).*

### Q4: Who is responsible? And why is this called "human-induced" climate change?

It turns out the answer is mostly us. That is exactly why we call it human-induced climate change. 

But how do we actually know it is people, and not just nature doing its own thing? Well, it is like looking at a beautifully simple puzzle where all the pieces fit perfectly. 

First, look at the timing. For thousands of years, the temperature of our air stayed remarkably steady. It only started heating up the moment we began running factories, power plants, and cars everywhere. The clock matches our actions perfectly.

Second, the math matches up too. The extra carbon dioxide gas now floating in our air is almost exactly the same amount as all the coal, oil, and gas we have dug up and burned. The numbers line up perfectly.

Finally, nature itself is behaving just as it always has. The sun isn't burning any hotter, and volcanoes are not puffing out any more gas than usual. Nature is just doing its normal job.

So, when you look at the timing and the math, all the paths lead straight back to us. By burning these fuels to run our modern world, we are changing our air.

Which leads us to the next question: how exactly are we releasing all these extra carbon emissions?

*Sources: IPCC AR6 WG1 Ch.3 (attribution); Global Carbon Project, 2024.*

### Q5: So how exactly are we doing it? What are all the ways?

The absolute biggest way, by far, is simple burning. We set coal, oil, and gas on fire to do three main things: make electricity, run our transport like cars and planes, and power our factories. Every time we spark up a fire, carbon dioxide gas goes floating up into our air. In fact, this single act of burning fuel is responsible for about three out of every four tonnes of those warming gases. So, burning fuel is the main culprit here.

But is that the whole story? Not quite. 

There are a few other big ways we release carbon emissions. For example, when we cut down forests, the carbon dioxide stored inside the trees escapes into the air. And to make things worse, we lose the very plants that were cleaning our air for free! 

Farming adds a surprising amount too. Cattle burp out a powerful warming gas called methane, flooded rice fields release even more, and crop fertilizers add another strong gas. Even making concrete lets out carbon dioxide, because we have to bake limestone rocks to make cement. 

So while burning fuel is the main cause, our forests, farms, and cement factories all add their own shares to the pile. 

Which leads us to the most important puzzle of all: how do we stop it?

*Sources: Global Carbon Budget, 2024; IPCC AR6 WG3; Climate Watch / WRI (sector shares).*

# Chapter 2: Eye Opening and Getting Ready
*How we, as a world, plan to face this challenge.*

### Q6: So what do we do now?

To find the solution, we have to start with a very simple and beautiful fact: the Earth actually cleans up after us! Our forests, our soil, and our oceans are constantly working in the background, soaking up some of the carbon emissions we release. 

The problem is simply how much we make. We are currently dumping far more carbon dioxide into the air than nature can take back. So, the leftover gas just keeps building up in our air year after year. 

To stop the warming, we have to cut down what we add until it perfectly matches what nature can soak up. Once those two sides match, the pile stops growing. Scientists call this balance "net zero."

So, how do we get to this balance? 

The biggest step is to burn far less coal, oil, and gas, and get our energy from the sun, wind, and flowing water instead. Next, we must take care of our forests and oceans, because they do that massive clean-up job for us completely for free! And for the tiny bit of carbon emissions we absolutely cannot avoid releasing, we can plant more trees or find clever ways to trap it. 

It sounds simple enough, right? But no single country can solve this on its own. The air is shared by everyone, so this is truly a global challenge.

*Sources: IPCC AR6 WG3 SPM (net zero and mitigation pathways).*

### Q7: Since this is a world issue, did governments come together and try to fix it? Any solutions or plans?

Yes, and they have been trying for over thirty years! Way back in 1992, countries met at the Earth Summit in Rio. There, they agreed for the first time that they had to tackle this giant problem together. 

After that, came a long string of big meetings. In 1997, a deal in Kyoto asked rich countries to cut their carbon emissions, but it left out poorer countries, and some of the biggest polluters simply never joined in. Later, a huge summit in 2009 was meant to fix everything, but it mostly fell apart.

For a long time, there was a lot of talking but not much progress. You see, every country was caught in the same puzzle: if we stop burning cheap fuel, will it hurt our growth? What if we slow down while other countries keep getting richer? 

But finally, in 2015, they landed on a solution that actually stuck. Nearly every nation in the world came together and signed a major deal in Paris. That deal, the Paris Agreement, is where the real story of global action begins.

*Sources: UNFCCC (Rio 1992, Kyoto 1997, Copenhagen 2009, Paris 2015); IEA.*

### Q8: What is the Paris Agreement?

The Paris Agreement is a major global deal where nearly every nation in the world agreed on one clear, measurable goal: keep the Earth's warming well below 2 °C, and try to limit it to 1.5 °C.

And how do we measure that warming? We compare it to how cool our giant home was back in the late 1800s, before we began burning fossil fuels in a massive way. 

To reach this goal, the world agreed to bring carbon emissions down to net zero by the year 2050. It is a hard number and a clear deadline that the entire world can be measured against.

But how do we actually get there? The agreement itself does not give a single, mandatory plan. Instead, **the execution approach and targets are left completely to each country to decide.** Every nation must figure out its own path to help hit the shared goal.

But to make any plan work, we first need to look at who is throwing what into our air.

Which brings us to the real question. Who is doing most of the emitting right now, and where does India stand on this global scoreboard?

*Sources: UNFCCC Paris Agreement text; IPCC SR1.5 (net zero by ~2050 for 1.5 °C).*

In fact, the top three emitters make up more than half of the world's total carbon dioxide. China is far in front, putting out roughly 30.9% of the world's carbon dioxide emissions. The United States is next at 13.5%. India comes in third at 7.3%.

Also, if you look at all of history, India has added only about 3% to 4% of the carbon dioxide humans have ever put in the air, even though we are home to one in six people on Earth.

But here is the catch. India is growing fast, and our people rightly want better lives, like more electricity, travel, and comfort. Just imagine if every Indian used energy like the world average person. India's yearly carbon emissions would more than double, shooting up from around 3 billion tonnes to nearly 7 billion tonnes! 

These numbers show that the world's climate future depends heavily on the path India takes. And that means we have to look closely at our own giant home: what exactly have we promised to do?

# Chapter 3: Our Pathway to the Common Goal Gate
*India’s big promises and the real targets we have to hit.*

### Q10: So what exactly has India promised under the Paris Agreement?

Our pledges under the Paris Agreement have a big, formal name: "Nationally Determined Contributions." But let's just think of them as our national promises. India updated these targets in late 2022, making them much more ambitious. 

At their heart are three massive, concrete targets we want to hit by the year 2030, and one grand final goal for the future:

---

### Our 2030 Targets:

*   **First, we will cut our emissions intensity by 45% compared to 2005.** 
    Now, this sounds like a big, scary phrase, but let's break it down. How does the economy have anything to do with smoke? 
    Well, to make money in a country, you have to run machines, build roads, and run factories. Every time a rupee of wealth is made, some smoke is released. You can think of it as a little carbon footprint attached to every single rupee we earn! 
    If we want to get richer and build more homes, we will naturally make more rupees. If we simply promised to stop making smoke, we would have to stop growing, and our people would stay poor. 
    So instead, we promised to make each rupee much "cleaner." We committed to releasing 45% less carbon dioxide for every single rupee of wealth we create, compared to how much smoke a rupee cost us in 2005. It is like finding a clever way to run our toy factories so we make the exact same toys, but burn half the coal to do it!
*   **Second, we will ensure that 50% of our total power capacity comes from clean sources.** This includes solar, wind, water, and nuclear energy.
*   **Third, we will plant enough forests to soak up 2.5 to 3 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide.** This gives us a massive, fresh green clean-up sponge.

---

### Our 2070 Target:

*   **Finally, we will reach Net Zero by the year 2070.** This is our grand final goal. But remember, the rest of the world has committed to hitting this target by the year 2050, and we are saying 2070.

*Sources: India updated NDC submission to UNFCCC, August 2022; CEA.*

### Q11: Why does India need those extra twenty years?

To understand why we need this extra time, we have to look at who actually made our air dirty. 

For over a hundred years, rich Western countries burned cheap coal and oil to build their factories, cities, and immense wealth. Since the late 1800s, humans have thrown about **1.5 trillion tonnes** of carbon dioxide into our shared air. 

*   **The United States** threw in about **25%** of that giant pile of smoke.
*   **Europe** threw in another **22%**.
*   **But India?** Even though we are home to one out of every six people on Earth, we only threw in a tiny **3% to 4%** of that total!

So, asking everyone to stop burning fuel at the exact same time feels deeply unfair. The West already built their rich lives. India is still building basic roads, schools, and homes for our people. 

The Paris Agreement actually understands this. That is why rich countries must reach net zero by the year 2050, but India has until 2070. This gives us some extra room to grow.

*Sources: UNFCCC (Common but Differentiated Responsibilities principle); Global Carbon Project (historical cumulative emissions, 1750-2023).*

### Q12: So we have an extra twenty years, right? Are we at ease?

Not even close! We cannot simply sit back and wait. 

Here is the hard, physical truth: if our air keeps getting hotter, Asian countries will be the worst hit, and we are right in the middle of them. 

But why is Asia in so much danger? It is a mix of geography and crowded cities. Most Asian countries sit in the warmest tropical zones of our giant home, rely heavily on a single seasonal monsoon rain to grow their food, and have millions of people packed into crowded coastal cities right at sea level. 

If the average temperature of our giant home rises by just 2 degrees, our daily life will change dramatically. We will face extreme heatwaves where the air feels like a furnace, making it dangerous for our people to work outside. Our monsoons will become wild and unpredictable, bringing terrible droughts one month and floods that wash away our crops the next. And as the oceans rise, our crowded coastal cities like Mumbai and Chennai will face dangerous seawater flooding.

We are doing it to save our own families, our own fields, and our own future: and the faster we do it, the better it is.

*Sources: IPCC AR6 WG2 (vulnerability of developing nations, regional impacts); India National Action Plan on Climate Change.*

# Chapter 4: Breaking down the problem!

### Q13: Fine, but where are all these carbon emissions actually coming from?

To solve a problem, we first have to map the battlefield. Let us look at the latest full data from the year 2023. 

If you look at the entire world, making electricity is the biggest source of carbon emissions at 33%. Transport comes next at 19%, followed by factories at 18%, farming at 12%, and buildings at 18%. That is the global pie. 

But India's pie is cut quite differently. Let us look at our five main slices:

*   **Power is 37.5% of our carbon emissions.** This is our biggest slice by far. The reason is coal. Roughly seven out of every ten units of electricity in India are made by burning coal, compared to just three in ten worldwide.
*   **Industry is 21.7% of our carbon emissions.** This includes making steel, cement, bricks, fertilizers, and chemicals. A developing country that is still building its roads and cities needs massive amounts of all five.
*   **Farming is 19.5% of our carbon emissions.** This is well above the world average. The reason is simple. India has the largest cattle herd on Earth, and our flooded rice fields release a lot of methane gas.
*   **Buildings, waste, and gas leaks are 12.1% of our carbon emissions.** Now, you might be thinking, "Wait, what about my air conditioner and my lights?" Leave those aside! If a machine plugs into a wall, its carbon dioxide was already counted at the power station. This slice is only about what a building burns *inside its own walls*, like the cooking gas in our kitchens, the diesel generators chugging behind small shops, the wet rubbish rotting in our city dumps, and the sewage rotting under our streets.
*   **Transport is just 9.2% of our carbon emissions.** This is barely half of the world average. Most Indian families do not own a car, and we mostly travel on two wheels, buses, or trains.

So, India's carbon emissions are not spread out evenly. One giant slice stands well above the rest: power at 37.5%. 

Nothing else even comes close. Think about this. Even if we magically made every single car, bus, truck, and train in India run completely clean tomorrow, we would only erase 9.2% of our carbon emissions. It would be like trying to empty a flooded room with a teaspoon while the main tap is still wide open! 

The power sector alone is the giant tap that decides if India wins or loses this battle. 

Which raises a massive question. How are we going to replace each of these dirty sources?

*Sources: Climate Watch / World Resources Institute sector data for 2023, via Our World in Data, retrieved 7 Aug 2026.*

### Q14: Wait, doesn't nature clean some of this up for us? How much do our forests and soil actually absorb?

You might remember that we just excluded "land use" from our big pie chart. There is a beautiful reason for that. Our land is not adding to the problem. It is actually our giant natural sponge!

Every single day, the trees in our forests and the deep mud in our farms are quietly breathing in carbon dioxide from the air and locking it safely underground. 

How much do they lock away? Our farm soil is actually our biggest champion, absorbing about 356 million tonnes of carbon dioxide every year. Our forests soak up roughly half of that amount. Together, India's natural sponge soaks up about half a billion tonnes of carbon dioxide annually. 

That sounds like a massive number, right? But let us compare it to the giant tap we just looked at. We are pouring over 4 billion tonnes of carbon emissions into the air every year. 

Think of it like spilling eight giant buckets of muddy water on your floor, but nature only gives you a sponge big enough to mop up exactly one bucket. The sponge is working perfectly, but it simply cannot keep up with the flood. The other seven buckets just stay there, making a bigger and bigger mess in our giant home.

Since we cannot just wait for nature to mop it all up, we have to go straight to the taps and turn them off. 

Which leads us to the next step. How are we going to reduce all those dirty sources and meet our targets?

*Sources: Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) soil carbon modeling; Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (biennial update reports on LULUCF sink).*

### Q15: Okay, we know the sources and the major ones among them, how are we going to reduce them and meet our targets?

Here is some very good news. Every single one of those sectors has a known, real-world solution. This is not a dream or some laboratory idea. These are real solutions that some country is already using today. 

But here is the catch. Every single solution comes with a real-world physical challenge attached to it. Let us look at them one at a time, biggest first:

*   **Power (37.5% of our carbon emissions).**
    *   *The Solution:* Solar and wind power, which India is already building faster than almost anybody.
    *   *The Challenge:* The real problem is on the supply side! You can turn a coal plant on or off whenever you want. But the sun only shines during the day, and the wind only blows when it wants to. We cannot control the supply. But people want to turn on their fans and lights the exact moment the sun sets at six in the evening. We have a highly unpredictable supply trying to feed a highly demanding evening crowd.
*   **Industry (21.7% of our carbon emissions).**
    *   *The Solution:* Run our massive factory furnaces on clean electricity, and make steel with clean hydrogen instead of coal.
    *   *The Challenge:* These furnaces need extreme, mind-boggling heat. Also, making cement releases carbon dioxide directly from the limestone itself, not just from the fire. So, even a clean fire does not save you from the rock's own chemistry.
*   **Farming (19.5% of our carbon emissions).**
    *   *The Solution:* Better feed for cattle, and growing rice without keeping the fields flooded all season long.
    *   *The Challenge:* There is no single giant factory to fix here. We are dealing with crores of small farmers, each making survival decisions about their own fields.
*   **Buildings, waste, and the rest (12.1% of our carbon emissions).**
    *   *The Solution:* Highly efficient air conditioners, cleaner cooking fuel, and better waste handling.
    *   *The Challenge:* As India grows richer and hotter, our demand for cooling is climbing much faster than the machines are improving. 
*   **Transport (9.2% of our carbon emissions).**
    *   *The Solution:* Electric two and three-wheelers, where India is already a world leader.
    *   *The Challenge:* An electric vehicle is only as clean as the electricity it drinks. Today, India's electricity is still nearly three-quarters fossil fuel.

Now, take a close look at that list. Every sector has a solution, and not one of them is easy. 

But do you notice the golden thread running through almost all of them? The transport solution needs clean electricity. The industry solution needs clean electricity. Even our buildings need clean electricity. 

Almost every road leads back to the exact same place! Power is not simply our biggest sector. It is the main gate that the other sectors have to pass through. That is why cleaning up our 37.5% power sector behaves like cleaning up 80% of our entire carbon footprint! If we leave power dirty, none of the other doors can open.

So, let us start where the smoke is thickest, and where everything else is waiting: electricity.

*Sources: IEA World Energy Outlook and India Energy Outlook; McKinsey Global Institute, "The hard stuff", 2024–25 (the solutions and their physical limits); NITI Aayog; Ember, 2025.*

# Chapter 5: Big Problem, But What First?
*Let's prioritize and hit the biggest ones.*

### Q16: Electricity sector: emission sources, solutions, challenges, and progress against targets?

Let me start by telling you a riddle. I am going to give you two facts about India, and they are going to sound like they completely contradict each other. 

Fact number one: India has built more clean power machines than dirty ones. We have more clean capacity today than coal capacity. 
Fact number two: We still get roughly three-quarters of our electricity from burning dirty coal.

Both of these facts are completely true at the exact same time! How is that physically possible? The answer to that riddle is the whole story of our energy transition.

---

### 1. Where the carbon emissions come from

**Making electricity is 37.5% of everything India emits.** This is our single biggest source of carbon emissions. 

Let us solve our riddle by looking at how our power machines compare to the actual electricity they make:

| | Machines built | Electricity made | Of the sector's emissions | Of India |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1.1 Coal and lignite** | 42.1% | 70.8% | **~98%** | **~36.8%** |
| **1.2 Gas and diesel** | 3.8% | 2.5% | ~2% | ~0.7% |
| **1.3 Everything clean** | 54.2% | 26.6% | ~0% | ~0% |

Look at the difference between the first and second columns. Coal owns less than half of the machines, but makes seven-tenths of our power. Clean energy owns more than half the machines, but makes only one-quarter of our power.

Why is there such a massive gap? 

Think of a coal plant like a tap in your kitchen, and a solar farm like rain. You can turn on a tap whenever you want water, day or night. But rain only comes when the clouds decide to rain. In India, the sun shines bright and strong for only about a fifth of the hours in a year. So, more than half of our beautiful new clean machines sit idle and asleep most of the day, while coal just keeps chugging along, carrying the heavy load.

On top of this, our wires are old and overloaded. We suffer huge wire leaks—what engineers call "transmission losses"—losing nearly 20% of our power before it even reaches a home!

So, our power sector releases about 124 crore tonnes of carbon dioxide every year, and almost all of it comes from coal.

---

### 2. The Beautiful Solutions

The good news is that we know exactly how to fix this! There are four clear, simple solutions that can replace that coal:

*   **Build more solar and wind power:** We can build massive fields of solar panels and wind turbines. This is the cheapest power in India, and we can build them very fast.
*   **Put giant batteries next to them:** A battery acts like a giant bucket. It catches the spare clean sunshine at noon, stores it, and pours it out into our wires at night. We know this works! Just recently, giant batteries supplied over 40% of California's entire power grid during their evening rush hour.
*   **Build stronger wires and fix the leaks:** We can build a massive new web of wires to connect clean power from empty deserts directly to our crowded cities, and fix our old local wires so we stop losing power to transmission losses.
*   **Move our demand to the daytime:** We can convince farmers and factories to run their heavy pumps and machines in the afternoon when solar power is cheap and plentiful, instead of at night.

---

### 3. Look at Our Progress!

If you look at our actual report card, India is doing an incredible job building these solutions.

*   **Clean machines are growing fast:** We have built over 297,000 megawatts of clean machines. That is 54% of our total, hitting our Paris capacity pledge four years early!
*   **Coal generation actually fell:** In 2025, the power we made from coal dropped slightly for the first time outside of the pandemic.
*   **Batteries are starting to scale:** The giant buckets are arriving. More than half of every battery on India's grid sits at a single, massive site in Khavda, Gujarat, built by Energy Group.

---

### 4. Progress against targets

| | Target | Where India is | Progress |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4.1** | 50% clean machines by 2030 | 54.18% | **Met, 4 years early** |
| **4.2** | 500 gigawatts clean by 2030 | 297.4 | **59%** |
| **4.3** | Clean generation (49% by 2032) | 26.6% | **54% of plan** |
| **4.4** | Storage (411 gigawatt-hours by 2032) | 5.9 | **1.4%** |
| **4.5** | Net zero by 2070 (power near zero) | 124 crore tonnes, rising | **Not started** |

---

### 5. The Reality Check: Why are we still burning coal?

If we know the solutions, and we are building them so incredibly fast, why is coal still making 70% of our power? This is where we hit the hard, physical challenges of the real world:

*   **The Clock Mismatch:** This is our biggest wall. Let us look at a simple day in India. At noon, the sun is blazing and our solar panels are making massive amounts of power. But most of us are at work, so we don't need all of it. Then, at 6:00 PM, the sun goes down and the panels go to sleep. Their power drops to zero. Finally, between 8:00 PM and 10:00 PM, everyone gets home and switches on their fans and air conditioners. Exactly when we need power the most, our biggest clean machines are fast asleep! To keep the lights on, we have to fire up all our coal plants.
*   **The Battery Wall:** If we need batteries to fix the clock mismatch, our bucket is way too small today. Right now, India only has about 1.4% of the battery storage we will need by 2032. They are also wildly expensive.
*   **The Wire Roadblock:** We have clean power, but no roads to drive it on. Right now, giant solar farms in Rajasthan sometimes have to be switched off because there are no wires thick enough to carry their power away! And building new wires means digging up streets, crossing private land, and buying expensive copper. It takes years.
*   **The Habit Problem:** Moving our demand to the daytime is incredibly hard because human habits are stubborn. A farmer wants to water his field when it is convenient for him, and factories are built around the schedules of their workers. Moving our demand means changing the daily rhythm of crores of people.

---

### 6. The muddy water in the bucket

If we have built so much clean energy, why did the carbon dioxide inside one average unit of our electricity actually go slightly up recently?

Think of our whole power grid like a **bucket of muddy water**. 

You start pouring in pure, clean water from our new solar farms. Because of this, the bucket should get clearer, right? Indeed, that clean water should have pulled our average carbon dioxide down by about 4%. 

But our average carbon dioxide rose by 1% instead. Why? Because the muddy water sitting in the bottom of the bucket got dirtier. Imported natural gas became so expensive that we had to use more coal, and we had to push our existing coal plants much harder. The clean water we poured in was completely real, but it wasn't enough to overpower the dirty water.

India is not behind on building. We are behind on **timing**: on those four critical hours between six and ten at night, when the sun is gone and coal is the only machine left awake. 

Electricity is the main gate that almost everything else waits at. But our factories cannot afford to wait. And our massive steel plants have a much harder problem: even if they plug into perfectly clean power, they still make smoke. Why? Because some of their carbon emissions never come from electricity at all.

# Chapter 6: Reality Check
*The hard truth about building our cities and feeding our people.*

### Q17: Industry sector: emission sources, solutions, challenges, and progress against targets?

Let's play another game. Imagine you are baking a cake in your kitchen. To make it work, you need two totally different things. First, you need the *heat* of the oven. Second, you need the actual *ingredients*, like flour and sugar. 

Heavy industry is exactly the same, just on a massive scale! When we make things like steel or cement, we need intense heat, but we also use coal and rocks as the actual ingredients. 

That is why cleaning up our factories is so incredibly hard. Here, the fossil fuel is not just the fire. It is also the flour!

---

### 1. Where the carbon emissions come from

**Industry is 21.7% of everything India emits.** This makes it our second-biggest source of carbon emissions, behind only electricity. But "industry" is not one single thing. Two completely different problems hide inside that number, and telling them apart is the key to everything:

*   **Heat is an energy problem.** A factory needs fierce, intense heat to melt metal or bake bricks. Today, it makes that heat by burning coal or gas.
*   **Chemistry is an ingredient problem.** This is the tricky part. Coal is the actual material we use to pull the oxygen out of iron rock to make steel. 

Let us look at the four places India's industrial carbon emissions actually come from, biggest first:

| Where it comes from | Tonnes a year | Of India | The problem inside it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1.1 Steel** | 240 to 300 million tonnes | **6 to 7%** | **Both** (fierce heat, and coal as the ingredient) |
| **1.2 Cement** | about 246 million tonnes | **about 6%** | **Both** (limestone rock releases carbon dioxide directly when heated) |
| **1.3 Plastics and fertilizer** | 70 to 90 million tonnes | **about 2%** | **Mostly chemistry** (fuel is the raw material itself) |
| **1.4 Everything else** | the remainder | **about 7%** | **Mostly heat** (making bricks, glass, clothes, and food) |

Two of those rows carry almost all the weight. India is the world's second-largest steel maker. Yet, Indian steel gives off much more carbon dioxide per tonne than the world average. Why? Because we lean heavily on coal. India is also the world's second-largest cement maker. 

---

### 2. The Beautiful Solutions

The good news is that we know how to fix these factories! Here are the ways we can clean them up:

*   **Mix leftover ash into our cement:** Cement is made from a baked rock called "clinker." We can mix in leftover ash from power plants so we use less clinker.
*   **Melt old scrap steel:** Instead of making brand new steel from rocks and coal, we can just melt down old, broken cars and buildings using clean electricity. 
*   **Swap the flame for an electric heater:** For smaller factories that just need to boil or dry things, we can throw away the dirty gas burners and plug in clean electric heaters.
*   **Make iron with natural gas:** Use natural gas to make iron. It still makes carbon dioxide, but much less than coal.
*   **Make iron with clean hydrogen:** This is the dream fix! We use clean hydrogen gas to pull the oxygen out of iron rock. Instead of breathing out carbon dioxide, the factory chimney breathes out pure water!
*   **Put a trap on the chimney:** Let the factory run normally, but put a giant filter on the chimney to catch the carbon dioxide, and then bury it deep underground forever.

---

### 3. Look at Our Progress!

If you look at our actual report card, India is already doing an incredible job on the easy parts:

*   **We are world champions at mixing cement:** India already uses less pure clinker in our cement than almost anyone else in the world. We are incredibly efficient at mixing in leftover ash!
*   **We melt a lot of scrap:** We already meet about 25% of our massive steel demand just by melting down old scrap metal with electricity. 

---

### 4. The Reality Check: Why are our factories still making smoke?

If we know the solutions, why are we still the dirtiest large steel producer on Earth? This is where we hit the hard, physical reality of building a nation:

*   **The Scrap Metal Wall:** India is a young, growing country! We have not thrown our steel away yet. Our steel is still busy holding up our brand new bridges and homes. We simply do not have enough scrap lying around to melt. 
*   **The "Moving the Smoke" Problem:** You can replace a factory's coal fire with an electric heater. But if that electricity comes from a coal power plant, we didn't fix anything! We just moved the smoke from the factory's chimney to the power plant's chimney.
*   **The Empty Pipe Problem:** We cannot use natural gas because we do not have the pipes, and we do not have the money. Gas is incredibly expensive in India, and most of our giant steel plants are nowhere near a gas pipe. 
*   **The Hydrogen Dream is Too Expensive:** Clean hydrogen hits massive walls. It is wildly expensive, it needs incredibly pure iron rocks (which India does not have much of), and it needs clean electricity that runs 24 hours a day, not just when the sun shines. Finally, if you make this expensive clean steel, someone else will just make cheap dirty steel and steal all your customers.
*   **The Chimney Trap is a Guess:** Catching carbon dioxide is very expensive. But worse, once you catch it, where do you put it? India has no pipelines to move this gas around, and we are still guessing if the deep rocks in central India can safely hold it forever without leaking.

---

### 5. The honest reality

Alongside all of this, India actually plans to build roughly 97,000 megawatts of new coal-fired power machines. 

We are not replacing our coal fleet yet. We are stacking clean machines on top of a coal fleet that is still growing, because our demand for steel, cement, and power is growing faster than both!

Our solutions all deal with the fire. They are cheaper, faster, and available now. But only the expensive, slow, and barely-started ones touch the actual chemistry. We can buy the easy half of the industrial solution today. But the hard half has no cheap answer yet. 

Factories cannot afford to wait. But even if we magically found a way to make our factories completely clean tomorrow, we still have to feed our people. And our farms have a problem that cannot be fixed with a clean electrical wire.

### Q18: Farming: emission sources, solutions, challenges, and progress against targets?

India's largest single climate problem does not only come out of a giant factory chimney. To find it, you have to look at something completely organic. You have to look quietly inside the stomach of a cow! 

And unlike a power plant or a steel mill, there is no single boardroom to convince, no gate to walk through, and no one machine to replace. There are simply crores of small farmers, each running their own land.

---

### 1. Where the gases come from

Let us start with a quick correction. Almost none of farming's pollution is actually carbon dioxide. It is made of two other gases: **methane** and **nitrous oxide**. Both of these trap far more heat than carbon dioxide. Methane traps about 27 times more heat, tonne for tonne! So, even a tiny amount of these gases does a massive amount of warming.

Farming is 19.5% of everything India emits, making it our third-biggest source of warming. Let us look at exactly where these gases come from:

| Source | What it actually is | Tonnes | Of sector | Of India |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cattle digestion** | Methane from the stomachs of cows, buffaloes, and sheep | 223 million tonnes | 54.8% | **10.7%** |
| **Fertilized soil** | Nitrous oxide rising off fields after fertilizers go in | 94 million tonnes | 23.3% | **4.5%** |
| **Flooded paddy** | Methane from rice fields sitting under standing water | 68 million tonnes | 16.7% | **3.3%** |
| **Stored dung** | Methane and nitrous oxide from manure heaps and pits | 13 million tonnes | 3.2% | **0.6%** |
| **Stubble fires** | Burning the leftover crop residue after harvest | 8 million tonnes | 2.0% | **0.4%** |

To understand why these numbers are so large, let us look at the biology. 

Inside a cow's stomach, there is a giant, natural chemical reactor. When a cow eats tough grass, millions of tiny microbes break it down, and their digestion produces methane gas. Every time the cow burps, that methane goes into the air. Because India has the largest herd of cattle on Earth, this quiet, daily biology adds up to 10.7% of India's entire carbon emissions! One animal's digestion out-emits our entire transport sector. 

And why do flooded rice fields make methane? It is a beautiful chemistry puzzle! When you flood a rice field under standing water, you seal the soil off from the air. The tiny bugs in the mud run out of oxygen. To survive, they use a different chemical recipe to break down organic matter, and that recipe releases methane gas into the air for months.

By comparison, the crop fires that fill Delhi's air with smoke every November are actually the smallest line on the list. They are a serious health problem for our lungs, but they are a very minor climate problem.

---

### 2. The Beautiful Solutions

The amazing thing about farming is that the solutions are mostly just clever biology! Here is how we can fix each of these organic problems:

*   **Feed our cattle better:** We cannot reduce the number of animals in India. They are protected, and they act as the savings accounts for poor families. So, our only option is to change *what they eat*. Better feed, like green fodder and balanced rations, cuts down the methane made inside their stomachs, while giving us even more milk!
*   **Waste less nitrogen fertilizer:** Most of the chemical fertilizer a farmer spreads never even reaches the plants. It just escapes into the air as gas. Using slow-release coated fertilizers, spreading exact doses, and testing the soil first can cut this gas without reducing our harvests.
*   **Let our paddy fields dry out:** Since rice fields only make methane because they are flooded, if we let the fields dry out once or twice during the growing season, the mud gets oxygen, the bugs change their recipe, and the methane stops completely.
*   **Seal dung in a biogas tank:** If we seal manure in a closed tank, the methane becomes clean cooking gas, and the leftovers become high-quality fertilizer. This is a double win: it removes a farm emission and replaces fossil cooking gas at the exact same time.
*   **Clear crop stubble without burning it:** Instead of burning the crop residue left after harvest, we can use machines to chop it up and mix it back into the soil to make the dirt richer.

---

### 3. Look at Our Progress!

Before we look at the hard challenges, we have to recognize one massive, beautiful twist about India's farming:

*   **The Giant Sponge:** India's farmland covers half of our country. Because we keep putting compost and fertilizers into the soil, India's farm soil actually absorbs 356 million tonnes of carbon dioxide every year! That is more than double what all our forests absorb. So, the farm is both the problem and our giant natural sponge.
*   **Farming is not running away:** Between 2000 and 2020, India's farming emissions grew by just 18%, while our energy emissions shot up by 132%. Farming is not getting rapidly worse; it is just stubbornly stable.

---

### 4. The Reality Check: Why are we still making these gases?

If the biological solutions are so clever, why haven't we fixed this yet? Because unlike a single massive power plant, farming relies on the daily decisions of millions of individuals.

*   **The Fodder Shortage:** The better cattle feed simply does not exist yet. Green fodder has to be grown on land, and it competes directly with our human food crops for the same soil and water. We simply do not grow enough of it to feed 30 crore cattle.
*   **The Subsidy Trap:** Fertilizer in India is heavily subsidized by the government and sells far below what it costs to make. When a bag is that cheap, a farmer naturally thinks, "Why not spread an extra one just in case?" As a result, our fertilizer use is rising fast, and soil emissions jumped nearly 7% in a single year.
*   **The Water Control Problem:** You cannot drain a rice field you do not control. Letting a field dry out on purpose requires the farmer to decide exactly *when* water arrives. But most Indian rice farms rely on the unpredictable monsoon or on strict canal schedules set by the government. 
*   **The Dung Logistics:** The dung is not in one place. Gathering manure by hand every single day from crores of scattered sheds is a massive, exhausting logistical headache.
*   **The Stubble Clock:** Between cutting paddy and sowing wheat, a Punjab farmer has barely two weeks. Burning the field clears it in a single afternoon for free. Using a machine takes days, costs money, and needs a very strong tractor. The farmer is not choosing pollution; he is simply choosing to save his next wheat crop.

---

### 5. The honest reality

Behind all of these challenges sits a massive wall. 

Our steel sector has a few hundred plants. Our cement sector has a few hundred kilns. But our farming sector is made of crores of separate families, each making their own survival decisions every single season. Most of them have absolutely no money or cushion to fall back on if a new farming decision goes wrong. 

Which brings us to our next challenge. Even if we make our electricity clean, and find a way to feed our people cleanly, we still have to move them across our giant home. And our transport sector has a physical bottleneck of its own.

# Chapter 7: Let's Get Moving
*The hard reality of shifting from petrol pumps to power plugs.*

### Q19: Transport: emission sources, solutions, challenges, and progress against targets?

Transport looks like the easy one. It is responsible for only about a tenth of India's carbon footprint, which is less than power, less than industry, and less than farming. And unlike a complex steel furnace or a muddy rice field, everybody already agrees on the simple solution: run our vehicles on electricity instead of petrol.

But when you look at what India actually burns to move things about, a completely different picture emerges. Two-thirds of our transport fuel is diesel. And diesel is not what scooters and passenger cars drink! 

The vehicles that actually matter here are not the ones you see in the daily news.

---

### 1. Where the carbon dioxide comes from

**Transport is 9.2% of everything India emits.** This makes it the smallest of our five main carbon slices. 

Almost all of this carbon dioxide comes directly from our roads:

| Mode | What that means | Tonnes a year | Of sector | Of India |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Road** | Trucks, buses, cars, scooters | 279 million tonnes | 94% | **8.6%** |
| **Aeroplanes** | Flights inside India | 12 million tonnes | 4% | **0.4%** |
| **Railways** | Only the diesel engines | 3 million tonnes | 1% | **0.1%** |
| **Ships and boats** | Coastal and river craft | 3 million tonnes | 1% | **0.1%** |

Road transport alone causes 8.6% of India's carbon emissions, which is bigger than every other form of movement combined, several times over. 

But if we look at the actual fuels we burn on our roads, the real story appears:

| Fuel burned on the road | Who burns it | Tonnes a year | Share of road |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diesel** | Trucks, buses, and delivery vans | 184 million tonnes | 66% |
| **Petrol** | Scooters, motorcycles, and cars | 80 million tonnes | 29% |
| **CNG and LPG** | Gas autos and city buses | 15 million tonnes | 5% |

Diesel is the fuel of hard work. Petrol is the fuel of getting about. So the sector that looks like an electric-scooter story is, by weight of carbon emissions, actually a lorry story! 

Look at how lopsided this is: heavy trucks make up only about three percent of the vehicles on Indian roads, but they cause roughly a third of all our road carbon emissions.

Now, let us look at a major physical link. Our railways show up in the table as only one percent. But that is only their diesel engines. India's electric trains used 31.8 billion units of electricity. That electricity gets counted under our power stations, not under transport! 

At India's current grid, that electricity carries about 23 million tonnes of carbon dioxide. The railway did not make its carbon emissions vanish. It simply moved them to a different page in our ledger.

---

### 2. The Beautiful Solutions

The good news is that we know how to replace these fuels! Here are the ways we can get India moving cleanly:

*   **Take the diesel out of moving goods:** We can put our trucks on batteries, or we can move the loads onto trains instead. India is already leading the world here: 99.6% of our main railway lines run completely on clean overhead wires.
*   **Take the petrol out of two-wheelers and cars:** We can switch the vehicles to electric batteries, or change the fuel they drink. India is doing both. Petrol sold across India is now blended with 20% ethanol, made from crops.
*   **Make our gas at home:** We can make CNG and biogas from our crop waste and dung, instead of buying fossil gas from ships abroad.
*   **Find a liquid an aeroplane can burn:** We need clean biofuels made from plants or waste to run our aeroplanes, which is a fast-growing carbon emissions slice.

---

### 3. Look at Our Progress!

If you look at our actual transport report card, India is hitting some incredible milestones:

*   **The railway is almost fully electrified:** We have electrified 99.6% of our broad-gauge railway tracks (70,002 route kilometres out of 70,271). That is a higher share of clean rail than in the US, Britain, or the European Union!
*   **Our fuel blending arrived five years early:** All petrol sold in India is now blended with 20% ethanol, hitting our national target years ahead of schedule.
*   **Electric sales are rising:** Electric vehicles made up 8.6% of every vehicle sold in India last year, and 93% of those sales were electric scooters and three-wheelers.
*   **The electric auto is our quiet star:** One in three new passenger auto-rickshaws sold in India is now electric.

---

### 4. The Reality Check: Why are we still burning petrol and diesel?

If we are making so much progress, why are our roads still filled with dirty smoke? Because heavy transport and battery chemistry hit some very tough physical bottlenecks:

*   **The Heavy Truck Battery Wall:** Long-distance trucks are short of carrying room. A heavy truck doing long-distance trips needs so many batteries that they would weigh about **three tonnes**. That is three tonnes of paying cargo it can no longer carry! Plus, plugging in a fleet of these trucks requires an electrical connection larger than most small factories.
*   **The Grid Carbon Debt:** An electric vehicle is only as clean as the power grid behind it. A battery costs carbon emissions to build in a factory before the wheels ever turn. An electric vehicle starts life owing a "carbon debt" that it must pay back. Let us look at the math on our current grid:
    *   **The scooter is a clear winner:** It uses half the carbon emissions per kilometre of a petrol scooter. A daily rider pays off the battery debt within the first year!
    *   **The car is a fragile bet:** An electric car on today's grid takes **about seven years (65,000 km)** to pay off its battery debt. If you compare it to a highly efficient small petrol car (doing 20 kilometres to the litre), the electric car on today's grid actually **never breaks even at all**. It only becomes clean if our power grid gets cleaner.
*   **The Dung and Crop Waste Logistical Headache:** Gathering crop waste and dung from crores of scattered farms to make local gas runs into the exact same logistical nightmare we saw on our farms.
*   **The Aeroplane Lift Problem:** You cannot plug in a plane. A kilogram of jet fuel holds fifty times more energy than a kilogram of battery, and planes must be light enough to lift their own fuel. Finding enough clean organic waste to make biofuel for every single flight is highly difficult.

---

### 5. The main takeaway

An electric vehicle is only as clean as the power grid behind it. 

Every road leads back to our electricity. If we make our power grid clean, our scooters, trains, cars, and factories all become clean alongside it. 

Which brings us to the final, remaining pieces of our puzzle. What about the buildings we live in, the garbage we discard, and the final loose ends of our daily lives? Let us look at how we rewires our nation, and how these massive national bottlenecks can actually become a unique business advantage.
