# Q&A 01 — Feasibility, buyers, and who pays
Raised after Module 1A.

---

## Q1. We're at 10% — how do we do the other 90%?

**a) 10% is a stock number; stocks lag flows.** 10% → 100% over 28 years = **~8.6% CAGR**.
Solar has grown ~20–25%/yr for a decade; EV sales 30%+/yr. The required rate is not exotic.
The stock looks bad because the flow only switched on recently (90% of all BEVs ever sold and
60% of all solar+wind ever built happened in the last 5 years).

**b) The 90% is not evenly hard.** Two piles:
- *Credible S-curves* — solar, wind, batteries, EVs, 2W/3W. Commercially self-propelling in
  many markets. Need grid, land, supply chain — not invention.
- *Genuinely stuck* — clean H2, CCUS, primary green steel, cement, aviation, shipping.
  Need cost curves to bend before scale means anything.

**c) Replacement cycles do most of the work** (see Q2).

**d) The honest part:** on current trajectory the world probably does not hit this. Mainstream
assessments point nearer 2.5–2.7 °C than 1.5 °C. The report explicitly says its scenario is
"used here not as a projection." Its value is identifying *which physical bottlenecks* determine
how close you get.

## Q2. Who buys 1B EVs and 1.5B heat pumps?

**Reframe: nobody buys 1 billion extra EVs — they buy their next car.**

- Global car parc ≈ **1.3–1.5 billion**
- Global new light vehicle sales ≈ **75–90 million/yr**
- Average vehicle life ≈ **12–15 years**
- → the world sells ≈ **2 billion cars between now and 2050 anyway**

So the scenario's question is not "who wants an EV" but "**what share of the ~2bn cars sold
regardless is electric?**" Needing ~75M EV sales/yr by 2050 out of ~90M total = ~80–85% of new
sales. In 2024 the world already sold **>17M EVs, >20% of new car sales**.

Same for heat pumps: boilers die every **15–20 years**. The question is only which box goes on
the wall at the replacement moment.

**This is why Reality 7 (asset turnover windows) is the intellectual core of the report.** The
transition happens at replacement moments; miss one and you lock in 15–20 more years. Identical
logic for a household boiler and a 1,400-tonne blast furnace.

Two complications:
- **Turnover rate is a speed limit.** Going faster means scrapping working assets — expensive,
  wasteful, politically toxic. This is why decades, not years.
- **Heat pumps >> harder than EVs.** A car is a *product* decision. A heat pump is a *building*
  decision: radiator resizing, insulation, panel upgrade, hot water tank, landlord/tenant split
  incentive. Hence chronic UK/German underperformance while EV sales beat target.

*India note:* space heating is not India's problem. The equivalent rows are air conditioning,
water heating and industrial process heat. Rebuild this row entirely in Phase 2.

## Q3. Subsidy, or do households eat it?

Three payers, and the mix has been shifting.

**1. Household, via TCO not sticker price.** The relevant figure is the *incremental* cost over
the fossil asset you were buying anyway, netted against lower running costs. Indian electric
2W/3W already win on TCO with no subsidy — which is why India's EV transition is happening there
first, not in cars. Small/negative delta → adoption self-propels. Large upfront delta (heat
pumps, green steel) → stalls.

**2. Taxpayer, via subsidy. Real, but shrinking and fiscally capped.** China ended purchase
subsidies in 2022 (tax exemption now tapering); the US ended its $7,500 consumer EV credit in
late 2025; India moved FAME-II → PM E-DRIVE, targeted at 2W/3W/buses rather than cars.
Subsidy starts an S-curve; it cannot finish one — subsidising a billion vehicles is fiscally
impossible.

**3. Manufacturer / utility, via mandates and standards — the real lever.** CAFE and fleet CO2
norms, ZEV mandates, EU 2035 new-car CO2 rule, boiler standards, RPOs. Costs the government
almost nothing; pushes cost to producers and into prices. India's cleanest example is not a
subsidy at all — **SECI reverse auctions** drove solar tariffs down through competition, and
PLI subsidises *manufacturing* rather than consumption.

**Underneath all three:** households do bear a large share, and where they can't, deployment
doesn't happen. Real equity problem most reports skate over — the people running 15-year-old
cars and old boilers are least able to fund the switch and their assets emit most.

## Implication for Phase 3

Energy Group does not sell heat pumps to households. Its advantage sits where the payer is a
**corporate, a utility or the state** — transmission, generation, ports, cement, data centres —
and where the constraint is physical infrastructure rather than millions of individual purchase
decisions. Structurally a better place to be. Build this argument properly once India numbers land.
