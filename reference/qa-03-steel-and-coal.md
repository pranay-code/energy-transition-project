# Q&A 03 — Are steel companies forced to choose coal because green technology isn't available?
Raised during Module 1C (Reality 7, the relining window).

---

## Short answer

**No. The technology exists and works. What doesn't exist is a bankable business case.**

Hydrogen-based direct reduced iron plus an electric arc furnace (**H₂-DRI + EAF**) is proven at
industrial scale. Sweden's HYBRIT delivered fossil-free steel to customers in 2021. The physics is
settled. The problem is money, inputs and timing.

---

## The four real constraints

**1. Hydrogen costs too much.** Green hydrogen is roughly 2–3× the cost of the coking coal it would
replace, per tonne of iron. Steel is a commodity sold on price with thin margins. A green premium of
20–40% per tonne has no buyer in most markets. The 2025 report update makes this worse:
**H₂-DRI projects were cancelled in Germany and the US in 2024–25** on exactly these economics.

**2. Not all iron ore works.** DRI needs **DR-grade ore** — roughly 67%+ iron content, low silica.
Only a small fraction of world ore supply qualifies, and it trades at a premium. Blast furnaces
happily eat lower-grade ore; DRI does not. So even a willing steelmaker may not be able to source
feed. India's ore is largely not DR-grade without beneficiation.

**3. It needs enormous firm, cheap, clean power.** An EAF plus an electrolyser is a very large,
continuous electrical load. That means clean **firm** power — not just a solar PPA — plus grid
connection and transmission capacity. In most locations that either doesn't exist or takes years.
This is the same grid bottleneck from Module 1B showing up inside the steel decision.

**4. You cannot pass the cost through.** ~600 Mt of **global steel overcapacity** (about 25% of world
capacity) is suppressing prices. When someone will always undercut you with blast-furnace steel, a
voluntary cost increase is commercial suicide. Green steel needs either a carbon border adjustment,
a mandate, or committed premium buyers (automotive is the main candidate).

---

## Why this collides with the relining window

Blast furnaces need **relining** every 10–20 years, and **~60% of world capacity is due before 2030**.
At that moment the operator chooses: reline (lock in 10–20 more years of coal) or replace.

If H₂-DRI isn't cost-competitive and financeable *on the day that decision is taken*, the reline
happens by default — and the window shuts for two decades.

> **Timing beats superiority.** A technology that becomes excellent in 2032 loses to a relining
> decision taken in 2028.

---

## What actually breaks the deadlock

- **Natural-gas DRI as a bridge.** Gas-based DRI is commercial today and cuts emissions ~50% vs
  BF-BOF. The same shaft furnace can later be converted to hydrogen. This is what the Middle East
  already does — it de-risks the decision.
- **Scrap and EAF.** Recycled steel via EAF is already low-emissions and cheap. It's limited by scrap
  availability, which grows as a country's steel stock ages.
- **Locate where the inputs are.** Build DRI where cheap renewables and DR-grade ore coincide, then
  **ship HBI (hot-briquetted iron)** to where the steel is finished. "Move the product, not the
  hydrogen."
- **Demand-side pull** — green steel offtake commitments from automakers, and CBAM-style border
  carbon pricing.

---

## India angle (for Phase 2 / 3)

India's position is genuinely different, and mostly favourable:

- India is **building new capacity, not replacing old** — the National Steel Policy targets ~300 Mt
  by 2030 *(verify)*. That's a **greenfield choice**, not a brownfield retrofit. India doesn't have
  to wait for a relining window; it is deciding right now what to build.
- India is the **world's largest DRI (sponge iron) producer** — but mostly **coal-based** DRI. That
  is both a problem and an opportunity: the DRI process route is already familiar, so the shift is
  to the reducing agent, not the whole plant.
- **Coking coal is almost entirely imported** (~85%, mainly Australia). This is the argument that
  actually moves an Indian board: green steel isn't a carbon story, it's an **import-substitution
  and energy-security story**.
- Indian steel demand is **growing**, so the 600 Mt global overcapacity problem bites less here than
  in Europe.
- The risk: every conventional blast furnace commissioned this decade locks in emissions to ~2060.
  **India's window is open now, and it is the only major economy where that's true.**
