## 1. The big argument (read this twice)

Most energy-transition talk is about **money** (how many trillions) and **policy**
(what targets, what subsidy). This report deliberately ignores both and argues:

> The transition is, at its core, a **physical** transformation. Billions of physical
> objects — power plants, wires, cars, boilers, furnaces, ships — must be swapped out
> in a few decades. The binding constraints are engineering constraints.

Why that reframing is powerful: engineering constraints do not respond to political will.
Energy density, flame temperature, CO2 concentration in a flue gas, mineral lead times —
these set the outer boundary of what any policy can achieve. Finance and policy decide
*how fast you move inside that boundary*; physics decides where the boundary is.

**The structure the report uses everywhere** — learn this, it is the skeleton of the whole
document and we will reuse it for India:

```
                        POWER SECTOR  (the hub — everything else plugs into it)
                              |
        +---------------------+---------------------+
        |                     |                     |
   MOBILITY              INDUSTRY              BUILDINGS      <- 3 end-use sectors
   (road, air, sea)      (steel, cement,       (heating,
                          plastics, ammonia)    cooling, light)
        |                     |                     |
        +---------------------+---------------------+
                              |
        RAW MATERIALS  |  ENERGY CARRIERS  |  CARBON & ENERGY REDUCTION
        (critical         (hydrogen,          (CCUS, efficiency)
         minerals)         biofuels)
                        <- 3 enablers
```

7 domains. The Aug-2024 parent report broke these into **25 physical challenges**.
This article picks **10 headline realities** out of that work.

**Date caveat, important for Phase 2:** the underlying modelling is the *McKinsey 2023
Achieved Commitments scenario*, built on data as of September 2023. It assumes countries
that pledged net zero actually hit it, and that warming lands at ~1.6°C by 2100. It is
used as a **yardstick, not a forecast**. When we rebuild for India we will be using
2024–26 data, so our numbers will legitimately differ — we must say so rather than
pretend to match.

## 2. Reality 1 — Today's energy system is high-performing, but flawed

The report's core move: before criticising fossil fuels, give them a fair scorecard.
Today's system is not primitive. It is *extremely good* at five things.

### The five beneficial properties (memorise these — they are the yardstick)

| # | Property | What it means | Benchmark number from the report |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Energy dense** | A lot of energy per kg and per litre | 1 litre of diesel moves a 2.6-tonne SUV ~10 km. Diesel has ~**50×** the gravimetric energy density (MJ/kg) of an EV battery |
| 2 | **Transportable** | Cheap to move energy long distances | Moving oil 1,000 miles costs ~**1%** of the oil's price. One average LNG tanker can power **40,000+ US homes for a year** |
| 3 | **Dispatchable** | Turn it up/down, on demand, fast | An open-cycle gas plant goes **0 → 100% in under 10 minutes** |
| 4 | **Capable of high heat** | Can hit the temperatures industry needs | Natural gas burns up to ~**2,000 °C** |
| 5 | **Chemically flexible** | Fuel *and* raw ingredient | **Thousands** of materials are derived from fossil molecules — plastics, ammonia, chemicals |

### The two flaws

| Flaw | Number |
|---|---|
| **Inefficient** | ~**two-thirds of all energy is wasted**, mostly in fossil conversion. An ICE car turns only **15–30%** of gasoline into motion |
| **High emissions** | Energy production + consumption = **>85% of global CO2**. Burning 1 kg of coal releases ~3 kg CO2 — which an average tree takes **40 days** to absorb |

### The nuanced conclusion (this is the part most people miss)

The transition must **keep the five properties while fixing the two flaws.**

And clean technology is *already ahead* on several of them:

- **Batteries dispatch faster than gas peakers** — a Li-ion battery responds in
  milliseconds to seconds; a gas turbine needs minutes.
- **Nuclear has a higher capacity factor than gas plants** — it runs a larger share
  of the year.
- **BEVs are 80–90% efficient** vs an ICE's 15–30%. Efficiency is a clean-tech *win*.
- **Heat pumps deliver more heat energy than the electricity they consume** (COP of 2–5),
  because they move heat rather than create it.

Where the real gaps sit:

- **Gravimetric energy density** (~50× gap, diesel vs battery) → hurts long-haul trucking,
  aviation, shipping.
- **Long-distance transport of energy** — electricity and hydrogen are harder and costlier
  to move over long distances than oil or LNG.
- **Very high temperature heat** — hard to reach electrically at industrial scale.
- **Chemical feedstock** — you cannot make a plastic out of an electron.

> **The single most important sentence in Reality 1:** the new system will *not* be a
> one-for-one substitution. Performance comes from **how the pieces are wired together**,
> not from any single technology beating diesel on every axis. Solar + battery + demand
> flexibility + interconnection can jointly deliver what one gas plant used to.

## 3. Reality 2 — Only about 10% of what's needed by 2050 has been deployed

The scale question. Under the 2023 Achieved Commitments scenario, by 2050 the world needs
roughly:

- ~**35 TW** of low-emissions power generation capacity
- ~**1 billion** EVs on the road
- ~**1.5–1.8 billion** heat pumps installed

Momentum is real: ~**90%** of all passenger BEVs ever sold, and ~**60%** of all solar and
wind capacity ever built, happened in just the last five years.

But measured against the 2050 requirement, here is where deployment stood in 2022:

| Domain | Metric | 2022 deployment as % of 2050 need | 2050 requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Power** | Low-emissions installed capacity | **~8–12%** | ~35 TW |
| **Mobility** | EV stock | **~3%** | ~1B EVs |
| **Mobility** | EV annual sales | **~0–10%** | ~75M/yr |
| **Industry** | Low-emissions steel + cement | **~10%** | ~5.5 Bt/yr |
| **Buildings** | Heat pump stock | **~15%** | ~1.8B units |
| **Buildings** | Heat pump annual sales | **~10%** | ~150M/yr |
| **Raw materials** | Supply of 8 critical minerals | **~10–35%** | ~400 Mt/yr |
| **Energy carriers** | Low-emissions hydrogen | **<1%** | >50 Mt/yr |
| **Carbon** | Point-source CO2 captured | **<1%** | ~4,200 Mt/yr |

Two takeaways:

1. **The average is ~10%.** Not 50%. Not 30%. The transition is in its *opening* phase,
   not its middle.
2. **The spread matters more than the average.** Heat pumps at 15% and hydrogen at <1%
   are not the same problem. Where deployment is near zero (clean H2, CCUS), the issue is
   that the technology-plus-business-model does not yet work. Where it is at 10–15%
   (heat pumps, RE capacity), the issue is *rate of scaling* — supply chains, grids,
   installers, permits.

Note also: today the world makes ~**90 Mt of hydrogen a year** and **less than 1%** of it
is low-emissions. The clean hydrogen problem is not "build new demand" — the demand already
exists. It is "replace an existing 90 Mt grey-hydrogen industry."

## 4. How Realities 1 and 2 connect

Reality 1 says: *the target you must hit is high — the incumbent system is genuinely good.*
Reality 2 says: *you are ~10% of the way there, with 25 years left.*

Together they set up the rest of the report, which is essentially the answer to:
**"Given that, where exactly does the physics bite?"** Realities 3–10 are the specific
pain points — power system shape (3), winter peaks (4), grid intensity vs EVs (5),
industrial heat (6), asset replacement windows (7), CO2 concentration (8), hydrogen
conversion losses (9), mineral lead times (10).

## 6. India hook (parked for Phase 2)

- India's grid is materially different from the report's implicit OECD default —
  ~75% fossil generation (the report itself calls this out in Reality 5).
- India's building energy problem is **cooling-led**, not heating-led. Reality 4 will need
  to be inverted for India — that is a genuine analytical contribution, not a translation.
- India's "energy dense / transportable" constraint shows up in coal logistics
  and in imported crude/LNG dependence, not in LNG tanker economics.
