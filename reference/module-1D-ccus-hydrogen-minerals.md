## Reality 8 — Carbon capture works, but not where we need it most

CCUS = capture CO2, then use it or store it underground.

It has worked for decades — but only where the CO2 is **already concentrated**, like natural gas
processing or ethanol plants. There, the CO2 comes out nearly pure, so it's cheap to grab.

**The problem: most emissions are dilute.**

| Tier | CO2 in the exhaust | Examples | Capture cost, $/t |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High** | ~95–100% | Gas processing (100%), ethanol (~95%), ammonia, high-purity H2 | **~18–35** |
| **Intermediate** | ~14–26% | Iron and steel (~26%), cement (~21%), low-purity H2 (~16%), pulp and paper (~14%) | **~58–80** |
| **Low** | ~4–14% | Coal power (~14%), petrochemicals, biomass power, gas power (~4%) | **~74–117** |
| **Diffuse** | ~0.04% (open air) | Cars, buildings | Not capturable at source |

Capturing dilute CO2 is **three to four times more expensive** than the easy cases — about $65–75 a
tonne at a cement kiln or coal plant against about $20 where the stream is already pure. You need
more energy and more equipment to separate a small amount of CO2 from a large volume of hot
exhaust gas.

> **Read this table carefully — the earlier version of it was wrong.** MGI's Exhibit 40 puts a
> bubble next to each tier showing **what share of US emissions that tier makes** — `<5` for high,
> `<10` for intermediate, `~35` for low, `>50` for diffuse. Those are emission shares, **not** gas
> strengths. An earlier version of this table read them as concentrations and mapped them to the
> tiers in reverse, so every row was wrong. The figures above are read off the bars of Exhibit 40
> (full report, p. 170) and cross-checked against MGI's own text, which puts steel at "20 to 30
> percent" and cement flue gas at "about 20 to 30 percent". The costs are read off the chart, not
> printed as numbers anywhere in the text.

Two more things must follow capture: the CO2 has to be **transported**, and **stored** — and
storage capacity itself needs to expand a lot.

**Takeaway:** CCUS today works in the places that matter least. To matter, it must get cheap in
the dilute cases — cement and power. That is a technology cost problem, not a deployment problem.

## Reality 9 — Hydrogen is useful, but leaky with energy

**What hydrogen is good at:**
- Lots of energy per kilogram (good for long-range transport, long-duration storage)
- Works as a **feedstock** — a molecule, so it can replace fossil molecules in steel and chemicals
- Can deliver **high-temperature heat**

**What it's bad at: efficiency.** Every conversion step loses energy — making it, compressing it,
moving it, converting it back.

> **40–75% of the energy is lost** when hydrogen is used for power, industrial heat, or transport.

The clearest comparison:

| | Electricity → wheels |
|---|---|
| Battery EV | **80–90%** |
| Hydrogen fuel-cell vehicle | **25–35%** |

So a hydrogen car wastes up to **four times** more energy than a battery car doing the same job.

**How to deal with this:**
1. Better electrolysers and smarter production/transport setups
2. **Move the product, not the hydrogen** — e.g. make hot-briquetted iron near cheap hydrogen and
   ship the iron. This is a big idea; hydrogen is horrible to transport, iron is easy.
3. **Be selective.** Use hydrogen only where its properties genuinely matter *and* nothing else
   works — feedstocks, very high heat, long-duration storage, heavy long-range transport.

**Takeaway:** hydrogen is a specialist tool, not a general-purpose fuel. Wherever direct
electrification works, it wins.

## Reality 10 — Critical minerals need a big, slow scale-up

Clean technology runs on minerals: lithium for batteries, rare earths for motors and turbines,
copper for everything electrical.

**Demand growth by 2030 (vs 2022):**

| Mineral | Use | Growth |
|---|---|---|
| Lithium | Batteries | **~7×** |
| Dysprosium & terbium | Magnets | **~4×** |
| Neodymium & praseodymium | Magnets | ~2.5× |
| Nickel, cobalt | Batteries | ~2× |
| Copper | Grids and electrification | ~1.5× |

**The problem is not reserves — it's time.** There is enough in the ground. But a new mine takes
many years, sometimes **more than a decade**, to come online. Announced projects are not enough
to cover demand to 2030.

Expected gaps by 2030:
- **Lithium** — ~40% of demand unmet in the base supply case (~5% even in the optimistic case)
- **Dysprosium & terbium** — ~75% unmet
- **Nickel** — actually in surplus

**Concentration makes it worse.** For cobalt, lithium, graphite, nickel and rare earths, the top
three producing countries account for **over 50% of extraction — over 80% in some cases**.
**Refining is even more concentrated.** That's a geopolitical risk on top of a volume risk.

**Ways out:**
- Faster mining — new extraction and surveying technology, modular construction
- **Recycling** — recover minerals from old batteries and magnets
- **Substitution** — design the need away. Rare-earth-free motors could go from under 10% of
  supply to the **majority of new supply by 2030**.

**Takeaway:** the mineral problem is a *lead-time* problem. You cannot fix it in the year you
notice it. Decisions have to be made 5–10 years early.

## The full picture — 25 challenges

The parent report mapped **25 physical challenges** across the 7 domains, sorted into three
difficulty levels:

- **Level 1** — deploy technology that already works. Fewest physical hurdles.
- **Level 2** — known technology needs to accelerate, and infrastructure and inputs need scaling.
- **Level 3** — technology performance gaps, heavy interdependencies, transformation barely begun.

> **12 of the 25 are Level 3 — and abating about half of energy-related CO2 depends on them.**

The 25, by domain:

**Power (1–6):** managing renewables variability · navigating nuclear and clean firm energy ·
scaling emerging power systems · flexing power demand · securing land for renewables ·
connecting through grid expansion

**Mobility (7–11):** driving BEVs beyond breakeven · going the distance on BEV range ·
loading up electric trucks · charging up EVs · refuelling aviation and shipping

**Industry (12–16):** furnacing low-emissions steel · cementing change for construction ·
cracking the challenge of plastics · synthesising low-emissions ammonia · heating other industries

**Buildings (17–18):** facing the cold with heat pumps · bracing for winter peaks

**Raw materials (19):** unearthing critical minerals

**Hydrogen and carriers (20–22):** harnessing hydrogen · scaling hydrogen's infrastructure ·
managing biofuels footprint

**Carbon and energy reduction (23–25):** capturing point-source carbon · capturing atmospheric
carbon · expanding energy efficiency

## India hooks (for Phase 2)

**CCUS** — India's realistic CCUS target is **cement**, which is chemically unavoidable
(calcination CO2) and where India is the world's #2 producer. Storage geology is the open
question: India has limited depleted oil and gas fields, and saline aquifer mapping is early.
Direct Phase 3 link — Ambuja and ACC are Energy Group.

**Hydrogen** — the National Green Hydrogen Mission targets **5 MMT/year by 2030** *(verify)*.
Per Reality 9, the sensible use is India's *existing* grey hydrogen demand — refineries and
fertiliser — not vehicles. Ammonia export is the other credible play, because ammonia ships
easily and hydrogen doesn't.

**Critical minerals** — India's weakest position by far. Almost no domestic lithium, cobalt or
nickel; near-total import dependence; and China dominates refining. Watch KABIL, the critical
mineral block auctions, and the Reasi (J&K) lithium find. India does have monazite (rare earth)
reserves but very little separation capacity. This is the challenge where India has the least
control — worth being honest about it in the final deck.
