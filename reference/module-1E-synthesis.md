# Module 1E — Phase 1 synthesis: the whole argument on one page

Source: MGI, *Ten physical realities the energy transition must tackle*, April 2025 (Modules 1A–1D).

---

## The core argument in five sentences

1. Today's fossil energy system has **five properties** we depend on — energy dense, transportable,
   dispatchable, capable of high heat, chemically flexible — and **two fatal flaws**: it wastes about
   two-thirds of the energy it produces, and it emits over 85% of global CO₂.
2. The transition is an attempt to keep the five properties while removing the two flaws, using
   technologies that are only about **10% deployed** against 2050 needs.
3. The route for almost everything is **electrification**, which makes the power system the hub of
   the entire transition.
4. But electrification runs into **physical limits** — grid intensity, very high heat, chemistry,
   asset lifetimes, mineral lead times — and where it stops, you need **CCUS and hydrogen**, which
   are both expensive and barely deployed.
5. So the transition is not one problem; it is **25 physical challenges**, of which **12 are Level 3
   and carry about half of energy-related CO₂.**

## The four ideas that actually run the whole report

### 1. The capacity–generation asymmetry
**You build ~3× the machinery to deliver ~1.8× the electricity.**

Because variable renewables produce only when the weather allows, and peaky electrified demand
consumes in bursts, the system needs far more installed capacity per unit of energy delivered.
Average utilisation collapses by ~27 percentage points.

Everything commercially interesting follows from this one line:
- Revenue per MW falls; the merchant energy-only model breaks
- Money must move **from energy to availability** — capacity markets, ancillary services,
  resource adequacy, contracts-for-difference
- Thermal plants don't disappear; their **job** changes from baseload to backup
- Flexibility (storage, demand response, interconnection) becomes the scarce, valuable product

### 2. The transition is lumpy, not continuous
Change doesn't happen when a clean technology becomes better. It happens when a clean technology is
**ready, affordable and financeable at the exact moment an old asset comes up for replacement.**

Those moments are scarce and scheduled by engineering, not by policy:

| Asset | Replacement cycle |
|---|---|
| Car | 12–15 years |
| Household boiler | 15–20 years |
| Blast furnace (relining) | 10–20 years |
| Power plant | 30–50 years |
| Building | 50–100 years |

**The longer the asset life, the more urgent the decision** — because you get fewer chances.
Miss a window and you don't lose a year, you lose a full lifecycle.

This is also the answer to "who's going to buy a billion EVs?" — **nobody buys an extra car; they
buy their next one.** The world will sell ~2 billion cars by 2050 regardless. The only question is
what share is electric.

### 3. Electricity is not a molecule
Two structurally different problems get lumped together as "hard to abate":
- **Heat** — an *energy* problem. Solvable with electricity if you rebuild the vessel, because the
  form of heat transfer changes (flame radiation vs induction/plasma). Aluminium proves >1,000 °C
  can be electric.
- **Feedstock** — a *chemistry* problem. Coal is the reducing agent in ironmaking; oil is the
  ingredient in plastics; ~60% of cement CO₂ comes from calcination itself. Clean electricity
  cannot fix this.

Feedstock problems are where hydrogen and CCUS are genuinely necessary rather than merely optional.

### 4. The grid is becoming the binding constraint
Panels are cheap and fast. A transmission line is a decade of right-of-way, permits and steel.

- VRE needs more wire per MWh: **smaller** assets, **farther** from load, **more distributed**
- IEA: global T&D volume must **almost triple**, growing >3%/year
- US interconnection takes ~5 years; ~70% of US lines are over 25 years old
- The 2025 update confirms it: **1.65 TW of solar and wind waiting for connection**, transformer
  costs up ~35%, lead times doubled to 2–3 years

> **This is the hinge of the entire Phase 3 Energy Group argument.** The world's #1 bottleneck is exactly
> the thing Energy Group builds.

## The 25 challenges, by domain

**Power (1–6):** renewables variability · scaling emerging power systems · flexing demand ·
securing land · **grid expansion** · nuclear and clean firm

**Mobility (7–11):** BEV breakeven · BEV range · electric trucks · charging · aviation & shipping fuels

**Industry (12–16):** steel · cement · plastics · ammonia · other industrial heat

**Buildings (17–18):** heat pumps in cold · winter peaks

**Raw materials (19):** critical minerals

**Hydrogen and carriers (20–22):** harnessing hydrogen · hydrogen infrastructure · biofuels footprint

**Carbon and energy reduction (23–25):** point-source capture · direct air capture · energy efficiency

> **12 of the 25 are Level 3 — and roughly half of energy-related CO₂ depends on them.**

## Where this leaves us honestly

- The **easy half is working**: solar, wind, batteries, EVs, minerals. These have credible S-curves
  and are increasingly self-propelling on cost.
- The **hard half is stuck**: green steel, cement, hydrogen, CCUS, aviation, shipping. These need
  cost curves to bend before scale means anything.
- On current trajectory the world probably lands nearer **2.5–2.7 °C than 1.5 °C**. The report says
  outright that its scenario is "used here not as a projection." Its value is diagnostic — it tells
  you **which physical bottlenecks** decide how close you get.
- For a business, that diagnosis is the point: **the money is in the bottlenecks, not the headlines.**

## The bridge into Phase 2 (India)

Five places where India's answer is structurally *different*, not just a translated number:

1. **Peak shape is inverted.** India's peak is summer-evening and cooling-driven, not winter-morning
   and heating-driven. India's equivalent of "COP halves at −10 °C" is the **solar sunset ramp** —
   generation collapsing exactly as the residential peak arrives.
2. **The EV segment is different.** The report's pessimistic India BEV finding is about *passenger
   cars*. India is electrifying **2- and 3-wheelers**, with far smaller batteries and therefore far
   smaller carbon debt and much earlier breakeven. Rebuild the analysis for that segment.
3. **Industry is greenfield, not brownfield.** India is building new steel and cement capacity rather
   than replacing old. Reality 7's window isn't something India waits for — it's open now. Best
   position in the world, and the highest lock-in risk.
4. **Distance is India's grid problem.** Best solar and wind (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu) sit far
   from the biggest load centres. India needs long-haul HVDC and ISTS on an unusual scale.
5. **Minerals are India's genuinely weak hand.** Near-total import dependence on lithium, cobalt,
   nickel; China dominates refining. Be honest about this in the final deck.
