## Reality 3 — A metamorphosis of the power system has to be at the heart of the transition

### Why power is the hub

Every credible transition scenario decarbonises mobility, industry and buildings mainly by
**electrifying** them. That means the power system has to do two contradictory things at once:

1. **Grow enormously** — absorb demand migrating in from cars, furnaces and boilers
2. **Clean itself while growing** — decarbonise its own generation at the same time

Global scale under the 2023 Achieved Commitments scenario:

- Global power system must **quintuple** in installed generation capacity by 2050
- Share of generation from low-emissions sources must **more than double, to >90%**

Note the word **metamorphosis**, not growth. The report is arguing the system changes *shape*,
not just size. That is the whole point of Reality 3.

### The Germany illustration (Exhibit 3) — six panels, one story

| Panel | What it shows | Number |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | VRE penetration rises as the system scales | **VRE ~3×** |
| 2 | Capacity ramps far faster than generation | **Capacity ~3× vs generation ~1.8×** |
| 3 | Flexible capacity needs a step change | storage + thermal flex + interconnections |
| 4 | Thermal capacity stays in the system as backup | **roughly flat**, shifting toward H2 / low-emissions fuels |
| 5 | Utilisation therefore falls | **capacity factor −27 percentage points** |
| 6 | And the grid expands | **transmission grid +85%** |

*(VRE = variable renewable energy: solar and wind, output set by weather, not by an operator.)*

### The single most important line in this module

> **Capacity grows ~3× while generation grows only ~1.8×.**

Read that again. You build **three times the machinery** to deliver **less than twice the
electricity**. Average utilisation collapses by 27 percentage points.

Everything commercially interesting follows from that one asymmetry:

- **Revenue per MW falls.** An asset that runs fewer hours earns fewer £/₹ per MW installed.
- **The merchant energy-only business model breaks.** If you are paid only per MWh sold, nobody
  will finance a plant that runs 5% of the year — yet the system *cannot function without it*.
- **So the money has to move from energy to availability**: capacity markets, ancillary
  services, reliability payments, contracts-for-difference, resource adequacy mechanisms.
  This is not a policy fashion; it is a direct consequence of the physics.

**Corollary that surprises people:** decarbonising power does **not** mean closing all the gas
plants. Panel 4 shows German thermal capacity staying roughly *flat*. What changes is its **job**
— from baseload (running most of the time) to **backup** (standing by, running rarely, ramping
hard). Same steel, completely different duty cycle and completely different revenue model.

### Why the grid has to grow so much

Three physical reasons VRE needs more wire per MWh delivered:

1. **Smaller** individual assets → more connection points
2. **Farther** from demand centres → you build where the sun and wind are, not where the people are
3. **More distributed** → the network must collect from many places, not deliver from a few

Globally, the IEA projects the volume of transmission and distribution lines must **almost
triple**, growing **>3% per year**, under a 2050 Net Zero Emissions scenario.

And grids are slow. The report's US datapoint: interconnection takes **nearly five years** from
request to commercial operation, and ~**70% of US transmission lines are over 25 years old**,
needing replacement within 10–20 years.

> **The grid, not generation, is becoming the binding constraint.** Panels are cheap and fast;
> a transmission line is a decade of right-of-way, permits and steel. Hold on to this — it is
> the hinge of the entire Energy Group argument in Phase 3.

## Reality 4 — Electrifying heat means managing much higher demand peaks

### The setup

- Heating and cooling = ~**85% of building CO2**
- Space heating + water heating alone = >**75%**
- Today mostly met by burning gas on-site; the main clean substitute is the **heat pump**

### The problem: electrification doesn't just add energy, it adds a *taller peak*

Demand for heat is violently concentrated. On the coldest hours of the coldest days, everyone
turns the heating on at once. Electrify that, and the spike lands on the power system.

US illustration (Exhibit 4), 100% electrified building heat, no demand management:

| Region | Peak vs today's peak |
|---|---|
| United States, national | **1.7×** |
| ISO-New England | **~3.2×** |
| Warmest states | ~1.0× |

And the peak **moves season**: the US flips from a **summer-peaking** system (air conditioning)
to a **winter-peaking** one.

### The detail that makes it genuinely nasty

> **A heat pump's efficiency falls exactly when you need it most.**

When ambient temperature drops from **5 °C to −10 °C**, a standard heat pump's coefficient of
performance (COP) **almost halves**.

*(COP = units of heat delivered per unit of electricity consumed; typically 2–5. A COP of 3
means 1 kWh of electricity moves 3 kWh of heat.)*

So on the coldest hour of the year you get a **double hit**: heat demand is at maximum *and* the
efficiency of the device meeting it is at minimum. The peak is worse than a naive kWh calculation
suggests.

### Why peaks matter more than energy

**A power system is sized for its worst hour, not its average one.** Capital expenditure —
generation, transformers, feeders, wires — is driven by peak. Adding 20% more annual kWh is
manageable. Adding 70% more *peak* means building physical plant that sits idle for 8,700 hours
a year.

Which is Reality 3's asymmetry showing up again, from a different direction.

### The mitigations

The report is clear the 1.7× and 3.2× figures assume **no management measures**. Options:

1. **Better cold-climate heat pumps** — units that hold COP below −20 °C already exist
2. **Hybrid / dual-fuel systems** — heat pump for 95% of the year, a gas or resistance backup for
   the handful of extreme days, so you don't size the grid for them
3. **Thermal energy storage** — heat the water or the mass of the building off-peak and coast
   through the peak hour. Shifts demand in time rather than reducing it
4. **Efficiency first** — insulation cuts the peak before you have to serve it

Note what these have in common: all four are about **flattening the peak, not cutting the energy**.
That is the correct mental model for electrified heat.

## How Realities 3 and 4 connect

Reality 3 states the general law: **a VRE-heavy, electrified system needs far more capacity per
unit of energy delivered, so utilisation falls.**

Reality 4 is one vivid instance of it on the demand side: a new electrified load that is
extremely peaky forces capacity you will almost never use.

Same asymmetry, two directions:
- **Supply side (R3):** VRE produces when the weather allows, so you overbuild and add flexibility
- **Demand side (R4):** electrified heat consumes in violent bursts, so you overbuild again

The system-level answer to both is identical: **flexibility** — storage, demand response,
interconnection, and a residual dispatchable fleet that earns money for *being there* rather than
for running.

## India hook (parked for Phase 2)

Reality 4 must be **inverted** for India, and the inversion is the interesting part.

- India's system peak is **summer-evening and cooling-driven**, not winter-morning and
  heating-driven. All-India peak demand reached roughly **250 GW in May 2024** *(to verify
  against CEA / Grid-India in Phase 2)*.
- India's structural equivalent of "COP halves at −10 °C" is the **evening ramp**: solar output
  collapses to zero at sunset precisely as the residential cooling and lighting peak arrives.
  Same shape of problem — maximum demand coinciding with minimum resource — different physics.
- That makes **evening-peak flexibility** (4-hour storage, pumped hydro, peaking capacity,
  demand response) India's single sharpest version of Reality 3, and a very direct route into
  the Phase 3 Energy Group argument.
- Reality 3's "grid is the binding constraint" point also lands harder in India than in Germany:
  the best solar and wind resource (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu) is far from the biggest load
  centres, so India needs long-haul HVDC and ISTS build-out on an unusual scale.
