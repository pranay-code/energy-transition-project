## 0. The spine — two different problems wearing one word

Industry is not one problem. It is two, and they need different answers.

- **Heat** is an **energy** problem. Fossil fuel is the *fire*. Clean electricity can do the same
  job — but the vessel has to be rebuilt, because a flame and an electric element deliver heat by
  different mechanisms. Aluminium proves it works: >1,000 °C, already almost entirely electric.
- **Feedstock** is a **chemistry** problem. Fossil fuel is the *ingredient*. Coal is what strips
  oxygen out of iron ore; limestone gives off CO₂ when cooked, before any fuel is lit. Clean
  electricity cannot touch this. **Electricity is not a molecule.**

Global split (MGI): **49% of industrial energy is needed above 500 °C**; 21% medium (100–500 °C),
22% low (<100 °C), 7% cooling. Iron and steel alone is **83% above 500 °C**.

## 1. Where India's industrial carbon comes from

India's industry ≈ **21.7% of national greenhouse gases** (Climate Watch/WRI 2023, via Q10).

| Source | Carbon per year | Share | Nature of the problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Steel** | ~240–297 Mt CO₂ | **~12% of India's CO₂** | Both — fierce heat *and* coal as the reducing agent |
| **Cement** | ~230–235 Mt CO₂ | **~7% of India's GHG** | Both — kiln heat *and* **56% from calcination** |
| **Plastics + fertiliser** | **~70–90 Mt CO₂ (OUR ESTIMATE)** | — | Mostly feedstock — the fuel *is* the raw material |
| **Everything else** (bricks, glass, aluminium, textiles, food) | remainder | — | Mostly heat, and mostly the easier low/medium heat |

> Rank by **absolute tonnes**, which are comparable; give percentages only as asides.

## 2. The six solutions, ranked — and the challenge behind each

Ranked by the Q12 test: **tonnes removed per year, at today's price and today's build time.**

**Solutions 1–4 attack the fire. Only 5 and 6 attack the chemistry.** That is the whole shape of
the sector: India can buy the easy half today; the hard half has no cheap answer yet.

### 2.1 / 3.1 — Less clinker in cement

**Solution.** Blend in fly ash (from coal plants) or slag (from steel plants) instead of clinker.
No new factory needed. India's clinker factor is **~0.71**, among the world's lowest.
Roadmap target **0.56 by 2070**.

**Challenge.** *India has already taken most of this win* — the cheapest tonne is largely spent.
The substitutes are **other industries' leftovers**, so supply is capped externally (fly ash
declines as coal plants retire; slag is bounded by steel tonnage). Push the blend too far and
concrete sets slower and weaker. And it **never touches the calcination half**.

> ⚠️ **Open inconsistency.** 0.71 is Power Line (2017). The NITI/CemNet roadmap runs **0.75 (2020)
> → 0.56 (2070)**. Different bases/years. Either quote the roadmap pair together, or quote 0.71
> alone as "among the world's lowest". **Do not pair 0.71 with 0.56 as one series.**

Maps to MGI challenge: *cementing change for construction*.

### 2.2 / 3.2 — Melt scrap in electric furnaces

**Solution.** Scrap needs no ore, no coal, no chemistry — just heat. **India already owns the
machines.**

| Route | Capacity |
|---|---|
| Blast furnace (BF-BOF) | **88.5 Mt** |
| Induction furnace | **74 Mt** |
| Electric arc furnace | **43 Mt** |
| **Electric routes combined** | **117 Mt — more than blast furnaces** |

> **The key insight.** India melts more steel with electricity than with blast furnaces. But the
> iron fed in is **coal-made sponge iron**. India's problem is not the furnace. It is the
> ingredient. (Source: Steel Secretary Poundrik via Daily Excelsior, Apr 2026.)

**Challenge.** *A young country has not thrown away its steel yet.* Steel sits in buildings and
bridges for 30–50 years. India consumed **41 Mt of scrap in FY 2024-25, ~25% imported**
(EY-Parthenon/CII/WWF-India). **Only ~25% of India's steel demand can ever be met from scrap**
(former Steel Secretary, May 2026). Expected **scrap supply deficit of 40–50 Mt/yr by 2050 even
under optimistic scenarios** — a shortfall *of scrap*, not of steel.

Maps to: *furnacing low-emissions steel*.

### 2.3 / 3.3 — Electrify heat below 500 °C

**Solution.** ~**43% of industrial energy is below 500 °C** (21% medium + 22% low) — boiling,
drying, steaming. Food, textiles, paper. Heat pumps, electric boilers, electric drives all work now.

**Challenge.** Two, one physical and one borrowed. **Physical:** a flame heats by radiation and
convection; an element/induction coil does not — so the vessel and often the whole line must be
rebuilt. **Borrowed:** it lands back on the grid at **~0.712 tCO₂/MWh**, so electrifying moves
carbon rather than removing it. *Industry's heat problem is the power question wearing a hat.*

Maps to: *heating other industries*.

### 2.4 / 3.4 — Gas-based ironmaking as a bridge

**Solution.** Commercial today, same shaft furnace shape India already knows, and **convertible to
hydrogen later**. De-risks the decision (Middle East model). **Use the Ministry of Steel's own route
table (§7) rather than a round percentage:** gas-based DRI-EAF is **1.40–1.60 tCO₂/tcs** against
coal-based DRI **2.70–3.10**. (The roadmap separately states ~30% lower on a full life-cycle basis
versus coal gasification and rotary kilns — a different comparison. Do not mix the two.)

**Challenge — two walls, and the second is the harder one.**

1. *India cannot afford the step.* Gas is **2.3% of India's electricity vs 21.8% worldwide**
   (Ember 2025); gas capacity has **shrunk from 25,329 MW (Mar 2017) to 20,122 MW (Jun 2026)**
   (CEA). **OUR ARITHMETIC:** 48.54 TWh ÷ 20,122 MW ⇒ India's gas plants run only **~28% of the
   time**. A steelmaker reads the same price sheet.
2. ***There is no pipe.*** Only **21% of blast-furnace capacity and 5% of coal-based DRI capacity**
   sit within 25 km of a gas pipeline (Ministry of Steel, §7.3). For nineteen coal-DRI plants in
   twenty the price of gas never even comes up. **This is a plumbing problem before it is a price
   problem — lead with it.**

> ⚠️ No sourced historical gas *generation* share exists in this library. **Do not write "fell from
> X% to 2.3%".** Use the world comparison and the capacity shrinkage instead.

Maps to: *furnacing low-emissions steel*.

### 2.5 / 3.5 — Hydrogen ironmaking (and green ammonia)

**Solution.** Hydrogen does coal's exact job — strips oxygen from iron ore — and emits water.
The same molecule cleans up fertiliser. Proven: **Sweden's HYBRIT delivered fossil-free steel to
customers in 2021**.

**Challenge — four separate walls:**
1. **Price.** Green H₂ **₹397–560/kg** vs grey **₹150–200/kg** (2026) — 2–3×.
2. **The ore.** Needs unusually pure (DR-grade, ~67%+ Fe) ore; **most Indian ore does not qualify**
   without beneficiation.
3. **The power.** Needs very large **firm, round-the-clock** clean power — Q12's evening problem
   arriving inside a steel plant.
4. **No one to pass the cost to.** ~**600 Mt of global steel overcapacity** (~25% of world
   capacity); someone always undercuts with coal-made steel.

International context: **H₂-DRI projects were cancelled in Germany and the US in 2024–25** on these
economics (MGI 2025 scorecard — steel is a *setback* domain).

Maps to: *furnacing low-emissions steel* + *synthesising low-emissions ammonia* + *harnessing
hydrogen*.

### 2.6 / 3.6 — Capture carbon at the cement chimney

**Solution.** The only thing that touches calcination CO₂. **Cement is India's realistic capture
target, not coal power** — see the concentration ladder below.

| CO₂ in the exhaust | Where | Capture cost, $/t |
|---|---|---|
| ~100% | Gas processing | ~20 |
| ~95% | Ethanol | ~24 |
| **~26%** | **Iron and steel** | **~70** |
| **~21%** | **Cement** | **~65** |
| ~14% | Coal power | ~74 |
| ~4% | Gas power | ~85 |

Dilute capture costs **3–4×** the easy cases. Cement at ~21% is bad — but roughly **half as rich
again as a coal chimney's ~14%**, and it captures cheaper (~$65 vs ~$74), which is exactly why the
ranking favours cement.

*Concentrations and costs read off MGI Exhibit 40 (full report, p. 170), 11 Aug 2026; costs are
chart bars, not printed numbers. Cross-checked against MGI's text, which gives cement flue gas as
"about 20 to 30 percent" and steel as "20 to 30 percent". **This replaces an earlier ladder that
was wrong in every row** — it read Exhibit 40's share-of-US-emissions bubbles (`<5 / <10 / ~35 /
>50`) as concentrations and mapped them to the tiers in reverse.*

**Challenge.** Still 3–4× costlier than concentrated sources. And catching it is only half the job:
India has **no CO₂ pipeline network and no proven storage site**. The candidate is **Deccan Traps
basalt**, which mineralises CO₂ into stone (so it cannot leak) — but **almost none has been drilled
and tested**; nobody has confirmed which spots can hold CO₂, how much, or for how long.

Maps to: *capturing point-source carbon*.

## 4. The framing that must survive into the payoff

> **India is not retrofitting. It is deciding right now what to build.**

The rich world's steel problem is a **replacement** decision — ~60% of the world's blast furnaces
need relining before 2030, and if the clean option is not bankable *on the day*, the reline happens
by default and locks in 10–20 more years. **India's is a first-build decision.** Best position in
the world, and the highest lock-in risk: every conventional blast furnace commissioned this decade
locks in emissions to ~2060.

Commercial angle for Q17 (do **not** leak into Q13): **Ambuja and ACC are Energy Group** (cement, where
capture is the realistic target); Energy Group is building **green hydrogen and green ammonia** at
Mundra/Khavda. Whether Energy Group has a **steel** position is still unverified.

Import-substitution is the argument that moves an Indian board: coking coal is ~85% imported, and
IECC warns India's steel expansion could lock in **$1.2 trillion of coking coal imports and
19.5 Gt of CO₂**.

### 7.1 Steel emission intensity by route — 2023-24 (Table 1.7)

**This table is the sourced basis for the beat-2 ranking. Use it instead of asserting an order.**

| Route | tCO₂ per tonne of crude steel |
|---|---|
| Coal-based DRI – induction furnace | **2.70–3.10** |
| Coal-gasification (syngas) DRI – EAF | 2.50–2.90 |
| Blast furnace – BOF | 2.20–2.60 |
| **Natural-gas DRI – EAF** | **1.40–1.60** |
| **100% scrap via EAF** | **0.55–0.65** |
| **India average** | **2.54** |

Country comparison (same report): India **2.54** · China 2.01 · Ukraine 1.68 · Russia 1.65 ·
Japan 1.61 · South Korea 1.35 · Europe 1.06 · Turkey 1.04 · USA 0.79. **Global average 1.91.**

**Why India is highest — the report's own four reasons:** limited scrap availability · natural gas
significantly expensive · low-grade coal *and* low-grade iron ore · integrated plants run on
**captive coal-fired power**, dirtier than the grid. *These are the government's own words and they
validate every challenge in §2.*

> **The single best number in the report:** in the coal DRI-IF route, **iron production using coal
> accounts for 72% of total emission intensity.** The ingredient, not the fire, is the problem.

Also: steel = **10–12% of India's total emissions**; sector uses ~**75 Mtoe**, about a quarter of
all industrial energy.

### 7.2 Green hydrogen in Indian steel — the real project ladder (Table 8.2)

| Project | What | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Jindal Stainless, Hisar** | India's first long-term off-take green H₂ plant — **78 tonnes/year**, used for *annealing* (a downstream step, **not** ironmaking). World's first off-grid green H₂ plant for stainless. Cuts ~2,700 t CO₂/yr | **Operational** |
| **JSW Steel, Vijayanagar** | 25 MW facility, **3,600 t H₂/year**, for use in DRI | **Under construction** (due Mar 2025) |
| **Tata Steel, Jamshedpur** | Injected ~6 kg/thm H₂ into a blast furnace for **4 days** → 10% less coke, **7–10% less CO₂** per tonne | **Pilot phase** |
| **CSIR-IMMT, Bhubaneswar** | Hydrogen plasma smelting — eliminates CO₂ entirely | **Laboratory scale** |

NGHM earmarks **₹455 crore** for steel-sector hydrogen pilots.

### 7.3 The gas bridge has no pipe

> Only **21% of existing blast-furnace capacity and 5% of coal-based DRI capacity** are within
> 25 km of a gas pipeline.

So 3.4 is **not only a price problem — it is a plumbing problem.** Strengthens the challenge
considerably.

### 7.4 India's DRI is coal — confirmed

**Rotary kilns are ~80% of India's 60.5 MTPA sponge iron capacity**, constrained to domestic
high-ash coal and low-grade ore. *Closes the open item.*

### 7.5 CCUS in India — the government says it plainly

- Theoretical CO₂ storage **395–614 Gt**: saline aquifers 291 Gt · **basalt 97–316 Gt** · EOR 3.4 ·
  coal-bed methane 3.7. Realisable **101–359 Gt**.
- **"There is no estimate on the true CO₂ sequestration potential in India based on actual site
  characterisation."**
- **"Currently, there is no policy for CCS."**
- **"There are no pilot projects in India"** for CO₂ transport and storage.
- CCS always carries a positive mitigation cost ⇒ steel gets dearer.

**Cement side (NITI):** process emissions are **~50%** of cement's total (CEEW says 56% of the
0.66 t/t — quote as "about half" or give both). CCUS could cut **35–54%** of cement emissions;
**CCS is ~95% of that potential** but needs storage mapping, land and transport — **"at least
5–10 years."** So **CCU (using the CO₂) is the near-term route, CCS the real one.**

**The pilot target:** **2,000 tonnes/day of capture (~0.67 MTPA)** and 2,000 TPD of utilisation,
**₹1,100 crore**, under the proposed National CCUS Mission. **Against ~230 Mt/yr of cement
emissions that is ~0.3%.**

### 7.6 Clinker substitution — the real ceiling, sourced

- **Blended cement is already 73% of India's production** (vs 27% OPC) — *India really has taken
  most of this win.*
- Cement currently uses only **~25% of the fly ash India generates** — but **demand for ash is
  expected to surpass supply by 2030–2035**, and ash availability **falls after 2050** as coal
  plants retire. Slag is bounded by steel tonnage.
- Next substitutes (**calcined clay**, calcium silicate) need geological survey and supply chains:
  **3–7 years**. **BIS standards** are input-based and need **3–7 years** to amend.
- Alternative-material targets: **5–10% by 2030**, 15–20% by 2050.

### 7.7 Cement's own bottom line

A combination of alternative fuels + clinker substitutes + CCUS gives a **cumulative 100–150
MtCO₂e reduction by 2030**. Clinker factor **0.75 (2020) → 0.56 (2070)**; net zero **2070**.
